<details>
  <summary> <b> 1. Introduction to Direct and Indirect Speech </b> </summary>


### 1. What is Direct Speech?

> Direct speech reports the exact words spoken by a person without changing those words.

> We put the spoken words inside quotation marks.

Example:
```
Ravi said, "I am happy."
- Reporting clause: Ravi said
- Exact spoken words: "I am happy."
- Quotation marks show the speaker's exact words.
```

```
SITUATION
A boy named Rahul speaks to his friend.

Rahul says, "I am happy."
The words inside quotation marks are Rahul's exact words.

Here is the sentence structure:
- Rahul says    --> the reporting clause.
- "I am happy." --> the reported speech.
- Quotation marks (" ") --> show the exact words spoken.
```
Remember: **in direct speech, we preserve the speaker's original words**.


### 2. What is Indirect Speech?

> **Indirect speech, also called reported speech**, reports what someone said without repeating their exact words.

Example:
```
Direct: Ravi said, "I am happy."
Indirect: Ravi said that he was happy.

Notice the changes:
- I changes to he bcuz Ravi is being discussed
- am changes to was bcuz tense changes
- Quotation marks are removed.
- that is introduced; it is often optional in statements.
- The reported words become part of the reporting sentence.
- The meaning remains the same, even though the sentence structure changes.
```
```
Direct:   Rahul says, "I am happy."
Indirect: Rahul says that he is happy.

Notice what changed:
- The word that is introduced; it is often optional in statements.
- The quotation marks are removed.
- The word I changes to he because Rahul is being discussed.
- The reported words become part of the reporting sentence.
- The meaning remains the same, even though the sentence structure changes.
```

### 3. **Direct vs. Indirect Speech**


| Feature            |	Direct speech	            | Indirect speech                      |
|--------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Exact words	       | Yes	                      | Not necessarily                      |
| Quotation marks	   | Used	                      | Not used                             |
| Pronouns	         | Original speaker's words   |	May change                           |
| Tenses	           | Original tense is retained	| May change, depending on context     |
| Sentence structure | Quoted speech	            | Integrated into a reporting sentence |

Important: **tense changes are not automatic in every situation**.

```
Direct Speech:   Riya said, "I am tired."
- Riya's exact words are quoted. [Exact Words]
Indirect Speech: Riya said that she was tired.
- Riya's words are reported in the speaker's own sentence. [Reported meaning]
```

### 4. The three main things to remember

When converting direct speech into indirect speech, check these three things:

1. **Pronouns**: `I, we, you, he, she, they`.
2. **Tense**: `am -> was`, `will -> would`, `have -> had`, and so on.
3. **Time and place words**: `today -> that day`, `tomorrow -> the next day`, `here -> there`,
   when the context requires a change.

### 6. The basic idea 

We can explain it in three simple steps:
**1. Direct speech**: Write what the person said, using quotation marks. <br/>
**2. Indirect speech**: Report what the person said, without quotation marks. <br/>
**3. Preserve the meaning**: Change pronouns, tenses, or other words only as required by the context and the relevant grammar rules.

Let's understand three terms: `reporting verb`, `reporting clause`, and `reported speech`.

`Rahul said to me, "I am busy."`

**1. Reporting clause**: This tells us who is speaking and to whom.
```
Rahul said to me
```

**2. Reporting verb**: This is the verb that introduces the speaker's words.
```
said
```

**3. Reported speech**: These are the actual words spoken by speaker.
```
"I am busy."
```

> Note: **The `reporting verb` is part of the `reporting clause`. It is not a separate, independent part of the sentence.**

### 1. What is a reporting verb?

> A reporting verb is the verb used to introduce or report someone's words, thoughts, questions, requests, or other communication.

Examples:
```
- said
- told
- asked
- replied
- explained
- requested
- ordered
- suggested
- exclaimed
```

Examples:
| Sentence	                                  | Reporting verb |
|---------------------------------------------|----------------|
| Rahul said, "I am ready."	                  | `said`         |
| Mother told me, "Be careful."               |	`told`         |
| She asked, "Where are you going?"           |	`asked`        |
| The teacher explained, "Plants need water." |	`explained`    | 
| He replied, "I don't know."	                | `replied`      |


### 3. What is a reporting clause?

> A reporting clause is the part of a sentence that identifies the speaker and contains the reporting verb. <br/>
> It may also contain an object or other information.

Examples:
| Sentence	                                                  | Reporting clause                 |
|-------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Ravi said** that he was tired.                            | `Ravi said`                      |
| **My mother told me** to study.                             | `My mother told me`              |
| **The teacher asked the students** whether they were ready. | `The teacher asked the students` |

Note: **The `reporting verb` is just the verb, whereas the `reporting clause` is the larger part containing that verb.**

```
Ravi said, "I am tired."
- Speaker: Ravi
- Reporting verb: said
- Reporting clause: Ravi said

My mother told me, "Study carefully."
- Speaker: My mother
- Reporting verb: told
- Object: me
- Reporting clause: My mother told me
```

### 4. What is reported speech?

> Reported speech is the part that contains the words or message being reported.

Examples:
| Direct speech	                       | Reported speech                         |
|--------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| Ravi said, "I am tired."	           | `Ravi said that he was tired.`          |
| She asked, "Where do you live?"	     | `She asked where I lived.`              |
| The teacher said, "Open your books." | `The teacher told us to open our books.`|

In **direct speech**, the reported speech is enclosed in **quotation marks**. <br/>
In **indirect speech**, it is incorporated into the reporting sentence **without quotation marks**.

Note: The reported speech doesn't always retain the exact original words. The message is reported according to the relevant grammar rules.

> Common **reporting verbs include `said`, `told`, `asked`, `replied`, `explained`, and `shouted`.**

### 1. Pronouns

> A pronoun is a word used in place of a noun.

Example:
| Sentences                                             | Pronoun  |
|-------------------------------------------------------|----------|
| Rahul is a student. **He** studies every day.         |   `He`   |
| Priya is my friend. **She** is helpful.               |   `She`  |
| Rahul and Priya are friends. **They** study together. |   `They` |

In narration, pronouns may change because the original speaker's words are now being reported by someone else.

The key is to understand **who is speaking, who is listening, and who is reporting the speech**.

### 2. The three golden(main) rules of pronoun change

A traditional grammar rule helps you remember pronoun changes:

**1. First person - `I, we, me, us, my, our`** <br/>
Changes according to the subject of the reporting clause.

Example: 
| Direct Speech                   | Indirect Speech                      | Pronoun Changes |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Ravi said, "I am ready."        | Ravi said that he was ready.         | `I` -> `he`     |
| Ram said, "I am happy."         | Ram said that he was happy.          | `I` -> `he`     |
| Sita said, "I love my mother."	| Sita said that she loved her mother. | `I` -> `she`    |
| The boys said, "We are ready."	| The boys said that they were ready.  | `we` -> `they`  |
| Ravi said, "My bag is new."	    | Ravi said that his bag was new.      | `my` -> `his`   |
| They said, "We are ready."      | They said that they were ready.      | `We` -> `they`  |
| Rita said, "My book is new."    | Rita said that her book was new.     | `My` -> `her`   |

**2. Second person - `you, your, yours`** <br/>
Changes according to the person being addressed.

Example: 
| Direct Speech                       | Indirect Speech                      | Pronoun Changes |
|-------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Ravi said to me, "You are helpful." | Ravi told me that I was helpful.     | `You` -> `I`    |
| Ram said to me, "You are kind."	    | Ram told me that I was kind.         | `You` -> `I`    |
| I said to Riya, "You are clever."	  | I told Riya that she was clever.     | `You` -> `She`  |
| The teacher said to the boys, "You are punctual."	| The teacher told the boys that they were punctual.| `you` -> `they` |
| Mother said to me, "Your room is clean." | Mother told me that my room was clean. | `Your` -> `my` |
| I said to Ravi, "You are right."    | I told Ravi that he was right.       | `You` -> `he`    |
| Ravi said to me, "You are late."    | Ravi told me that I was late.        | `You` -> `I`     |

We can notice that `you` does not always become `he` or `I`. It depends on whom the speaker addressed.

**3. Third person - `he, she, it, they, him, her, them, his, their`** <br/>
Usually remains unchanged, unless the context requires a different reference. 

Example: 
| Direct Speech                       | Indirect Speech                      | Pronoun Changes  |
|-------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|------------------|
| Ravi said, "She is intelligent."    | Ravi said that she was intelligent.  | `She` -> `she`   |
| Ram said, "He is honest."	          | Ram said that he was honest.         | `He`  -> `He`    |
| Sita said, "She is my friend."	    | Sita said that she was her friend.   | `She` -> `she`   |
| Mohan said, "They are playing."	    | Mohan said that they were playing.   | `They` -> `they` |
| Ram said, "I am tired."             | Ram said that he was tired.          | `I` -> `he`      |


### 3. A simple trick for exams

When you see direct speech, ask:
```
1. Who said these words?
2. Who was addressed?
3. Who is being talked about?
Answer these questions before changing the pronouns.
Then apply the tense rules
```

Let's solve
```
Direct: Amit said to Priya, "I will help you with your homework."

- Step 1: Identify the speaker.  Amit is speaking, so I becomes he.
- Step 2: Identify the listener. Amit is talking to Priya, so you becomes her and your becomes her.
- Step 3: Change the reporting structure and tense as required.

Indirect: Amit told Priya that he would help her with her homework.
```
We can notice that the **pronouns changed according to the people involved**, while **`will` changed to `would` under the usual backshift rule**.

### 5. Remember

| Direct speech     | Indirect speech rule                      |
|-------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| `I/we`            | Changes according to the speaker          | 
| `You/your`        | Changes according to the person addressed |
| `He/she/they`     | Usually remains the same referent         |
| `Will`            | Usually becomes `would`                   |
| `Have` completed  | Usually becomes `had` completed           | 
| `Said` to someone | Often becomes `told` someone              |
| `said` only       | often remains `said`                      |

**Notes**
> **First person: generally changes according to the speaker**.

> **Second person: generally changes according to the listener**.

> **Third person: generally remains unchanged**.

> Always identify the person to whom each pronoun refers before changing it.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 4. Rules for changing tenses </b> </summary>

<br/>

We will frequently be asked to change the `tense` when converting direct speech into indirect speech.

**When do we change the tense?**
```
                 Step 1: Find the reporting verb.
                                 |
                                 |
            Is it in the past tense, such as said or told?
                                  |
                                  | 
           Yes                                        No
            |                                          |
Usually backshift the tense.              Tense backshift may not be needed.
```
Example:
```
Past reporting verb:
Direct:   He said, "I am hungry."
Indirect: He said that he was hungry.

Present reporting verb:
Direct:   He says, "I am hungry."
Indirect: He says that he is hungry.
```

### 1. The golden rule: Tense Backshift

> **Tense backshift** means moving the tense of the reported speech one step back into the past when reporting someone's words, usually after a past reporting verb such as `said` or `told` or `asked`.


Example:
```
Direct: Ravi said, "I am busy."
- "am" =  present simple form of be.
Indirect: Ravi said that he was busy.
- Here, "am" -> "was".

Why does this happen?
- Bcuz the reporting verb said is in the past tense, and
the speaker's original words are being reported from a later viewpoint.
```

Note: Tense backshift is common, but it is not compulsory in every situation. We will see exceptions.

### 2. Complete(Master) tense conversion table

| Direct speech              | Indirect speech         | Example                                 |
|----------------------------|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| Present Simple             | Past Simple             | I work -> he worked                          |
| Present Continuous         | Past Continuous         | I am working -> he was working               |
| Present Perfect            | Past Perfect            | I have worked -> he had worked               |
| Present Perfect Continuous | Past Perfect Continuous | I have been working -> he had been working   |
| Past Simple                | Past Perfect            | I worked -> he had worked                    |
| Past Continuous            | Past Perfect Continuous | I was working -> he had been working         |
| Past Perfect               | Past Perfect            | I had worked -> he had worked                |
| Past Perfect Continuous    | Past Perfect Continuous | I had been working -> he had been working    |
| `Will + V1`                | `Would + V1`            | I will go -> he would go                     |
| `Will be + V-ing`          | `Would be + V-ing`      | I will be going -> he would be going         |
| `Will have + V3`           | `Would have + V3`       | I will have gone -> he would have gone       |

> **V1 = base verb, V3 = past participle.**

The key idea is that the **past perfect and past perfect continuous usually remain unchanged** because they are already past-perfect forms.

```

```

| Past tenses    |
|----------------|

**Past Simple -> Past Perfect**
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "I bought a car."
Indirect: Ravi said that he had bought a car.

Direct:   Priya said, "I completed my work."
Indirect: Priya said that she had completed her work.
```
**Past Continuous -> Past Perfect Continuous**
```
Direct:   She said, "I was cooking."

| Future tenses  |
|----------------|

**What happens to future tense?** <br/>
Future forms also change when backshift is required.

| Direct speech	                              | Indirect speech                                         |
|---------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| `will` -> `would`	                          | "I will go." -> He said he would go.                    |
| `shall` -> `would`(often)	                  | "I shall return." -> He said he would return.           |
| `am/is/are going to` -> `was/were going to` | "I am going to study." -> He said he was going to study.|


**`will` -> `would`**
```
Direct:   Aman said, "I will call you."
Indirect: Aman said that he would call me.

Direct:   Anil said, "I will help you."
Indirect: Anil said that he would help me.
```

**`will be + V-ing` -> `would be + V-ing`**
```
Direct:   She said, "I will be travelling tomorrow."
Indirect: She said that she would be travelling the next day.
```

**`will have + V3`** -> **`would have + V3`**
```
Direct: He said, "I will have finished the work."
Indirect: He said that he would have finished the work.
```

| Modal Verbs   |
|---------------|

**What happens to modal verbs?** <br/>
Modal verbs express ability, permission, possibility, obligation, and other meanings.

| Example                               | Direct speech   -> Indirect speech                     |
|---------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| `can` -> `could`                      | "I can swim."  -> He said he could swim.               |
| `may` -> `might`                      | "I may come." -> He said he might come.                |
| `must` -> `had to` (when obligation is intended) | "I must leave." -> He said he had to leave. |
| `will` -> `would`                     | "I will call." -> He said he would call.               |
| `shall` -> `should/would`, depending on meaning | Context matters.                             |

**Some modal verbs generally remain unchanged: `should, would, could, might, ought to`**
```
Direct:   She said, "I could solve it."
Indirect: She said that she could solve it.

```

### 4. Important exceptions

Tense does not always have to change.

**A. Reporting verb in the present**
```
Direct: He says, "I am tired."
Indirect: He says that he is tired.
```
Because `says` is present tense, the usual backshift does not apply.

**B. Universal truths and scientific facts**
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
Indirect: The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
```
The present tense is retained because this is a universal fact.

**C. A fact that is still true**
```
Direct: Maya said, "I live in Delhi."
- If Maya still lives in Delhi, we can say:
Indirect: Maya said that she lives in Delhi.
```
If you are simply following the standard backshift pattern in an exam, `lived` is usually expected.

<br/>

We'll learn how words such as `today, tomorrow, here, this, and these` may change when we report someone’s words.

### 1. Time expressions

When someone speaks, they use time expressions from their own point of view. <br/>
When we report those words later, the time expressions may need to change.

| Direct speech  | Indirect speech     |
|----------------|---------------------|
| `now`          | `then`              |
| `today`        | `that day`          |
| `yesterday`    | `the previous day`  |
| `tomorrow`     | `the next day`      |
| `last night`   | `the previous night`|
| `next week`    | `the following week`|
| `ago`          | `before`            |
| `last week`    | `the previous week` |
| `last month`	 | `the previous month`        |
| `next month`	 | `the following month`       |
| `a year ago`	 | `a year before`             |
| `tonight`      | `that night`                |
| `yesterday morning` |	`the previous morning` |

The best choice can vary with the precise time reference and context.

Examples:
```
Direct:   Rahul said, "I am busy today."
Indirect: Rahul said that he was busy that day.

Direct:   Neha said, "I will visit you tomorrow."
Indirect: Neha said that she would visit me the next day.

Direct:   Amit said, "I met him two days ago."
Indirect: Amit said that he had met him two days before.
```

### 2. Place expressions

Words that describe location can change when the speaker's original location differs from the reporter's location.

| Direct speech	| Indirect speech |
|---------------|-----------------|
| `here`       	| `there`         |
| `this place`  |	`that place`    |
| `come here`   |	`go there` (when the perspective requires it) |

Examples:
```
Direct:   Riya said, "I live here."
Indirect: Riya said that she lived there.

Direct:   The teacher said, "Come here."
Indirect: The teacher told the student to go there.
```

### 3. Demonstrative words

Demonstratives help us point to people or things.

| Direct speech	| Indirect speech |
|---------------|-----------------|
| `This`        | `That`          |
| `These`       | `Those`         |

Examples:
```
Direct:   He said, "This apple is sweet."
Indirect: He said that that apple was sweet.

Direct:   She said, "These books are mine."
Indirect: She said that those books were hers.
```
However, if the same object is still near the reporter, this or these may remain appropriate.

### 4. Example

```
Direct speech:   Priya said, "I will finish this work here tomorrow."
Indirect speech: Priya said that she would finish that work there the next day.
```
| Element	      | Direct	      | Indirect        |
|---------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Pronoun	      | `I`	          | `she`           |
| Tense/modal	  | `will` finish |	`would` finish  |
| Demonstrative	| `this` work	  | `that` work     |
| Place	        | `here`	      | `there`         |
| Time	        | `tomorrow`	  | `the next day`  |

### 5. When should these words NOT change?

The changes above are not mechanical rules that apply in every situation.
```
Direct:   Rahul said yesterday, "I will finish this work tomorrow."
Indirect: Rahul said yesterday that he would finish this work tomorrow.
- Here, tomorrow may remain tomorrow because the reporting happens on the same day as Rahul's original statement.
```
If you report this on the same day and the original work is still the same work.

So, before changing a time, place, or demonstrative word, ask:
```
1. When was the original statement made?
2. When am I reporting it?
3. Am I in the same place?
4. Am I referring to the same object or situation?
```
This helps you produce meaningful sentences rather than simply memorizing substitutions.

### 6. Practice test

```
1. He said, "I will come tomorrow."
- He said that he would come the next day.
- Will changes to would, tomorrow usually changes to the next day, and I changes to he.

2. She said, "I saw him yesterday."
- She said that she had seen him the previous day.
- Yesterday usually becomes the previous day; saw usually changes to had seen.

3. Ravi said, "These flowers are beautiful."
- Ravi said that those flowers were beautiful.
- Those is the conventional conversion of these, and are usually changes to were after a past reporting verb.

4. She said, "I live here."
- She said that she lived there.
- Here commonly changes to there when the reporting location differs.

5. He said, "I met her two days ago."
- He said that he had met her two days before.
- Ago usually becomes before in a past reporting context.

6. Must "tomorrow" always change to "the next day"?
- No, it depends on when the statement is reported.
- If the reporting occurs on the same day and the time reference is unchanged, tomorrow may remain tomorrow.
```

### 7. Quick revision

- **Time**: `today -> that day`; `tomorrow -> the next day`; `yesterday -> the previous day`.
- **Place**: `here -> there`.
- **Demonstratives**: `this -> that`; `these -> those`.

Remember: changes depend on the reporting time, location, and context.

### 1. What is a statement?

> A statement is a sentence that gives information or expresses an idea, fact, opinion, or feeling.

Examples:
```
Rohan is a good student.
Priya likes mangoes.
The teacher is explaining the lesson.
They have completed their homework.
```
When these sentences are spoken and reported, we can express them in direct or indirect speech.

```
Direct speech:   Rohan said, "I am a good student."
Indirect speech: Rohan said that he was a good student.
```
Notice that the sentence is a statement, not a question, command, or exclamation.

### Rule 1: Statements with `said`

When the reporting clause uses `said` and there is no object after it, the basic pattern is:

**Structure** <br/>
**Direct speech: `Subject + said + "statement"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + said + (that) + reported statement`**

Examples
```
Present simple
Direct: Amit said, "I play cricket."
Indirect: Amit said that he played cricket.
Changes:
- I -> he
- play -> played
- Quotation marks are removed.
- That is introduced but is optional.

Present continuous
Direct: Sita said, "I am reading a book."
Indirect: Sita said that she was reading a book.
Changes:
- I -> she
- am reading -> was reading

Future tense
Direct: Rahul said, "I will help you."
Indirect: Rahul said that he would help me.
Here, you becomes me only if the original listener was the person reporting the sentence.
```
Tip: Never change `you` automatically to `me`. First identify the person being addressed.


### Rule 2: Statements with `said to`

When `said to` is followed by the person addressed, we generally change `said to + person` into `told + person` when converting a statement into indirect speech.

**Direct speech: `Subject + said to person + "statement"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + told person + (that) + reported statement`**
```
Direct Speech:   Riya said to Mohan, "You are honest."
Indirect Speech: Riya told Mohan that he was honest.
- said to Mohan -> told Mohan
- you -> he
- are -> was

Correct: Riya told Mohan that he was honest.
Incorrect: Riya told to Mohan that he was honest.
```

### 4. Compare `said` and `said to`

| Direct speech	                     | Indirect speech                    |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| He said, "I am busy."	             | He said that he was busy.          |
| He said to me, "I am busy."        | He told me that he was busy.       |
| She said to Ravi, "You are right." | She told Ravi that he was right.   |
| They said to us, "We are ready."	 | They told us that they were ready. |

The key distinction is the presence of the person addressed.
- **`said`**: no object is necessary.
- **`said to`**: identifies the person addressed.
- **`told`**: normally requires an object when used to report a statement.


### 5. A complete transformation method

Let's solve one sentence

```
Direct: The teacher said to the students, "You have completed your work."

Step 1 - Identify the reporting verb: said to.
Step 2 - Change said to the students to told the students.
Step 3 - Change the pronouns:
         - You -> they
         - Your -> their
Step 4 - Change the tense:
         - Have completed -> had completed
Step 5 - Remove quotation marks and join the sentence.

Indirect: The teacher told the students that they had completed their work.
```
This is the standard answer when backshift is appropriate.

### 7. Practice: Convert the statements

```
1. Mohan said, "I am hungry."
- Mohan said that he was hungry.
- I refers to Mohan, and am usually changes to was.

2. Riya said to me, "You are intelligent."
- Riya told me that I was intelligent.
- Said to me changes to told me, and you refers to the person addressed.

3. The boys said, "We are playing football."
- The boys said that they were playing football.
- We refers to the boys, and are playing usually changes to were playing.

4. Anu said to Ravi, "I will call you."
- Anu told Ravi that she would call him.
- Said to Ravi changes to told Ravi; I becomes she, will becomes would, and you becomes him.

5. The teacher said, "The students have finished their work."
- The teacher said that the students had finished their work.
- Have finished changes to had finished in the usual backshift exercise.
```

### 8. Final revision

Remember these two patterns:
- **`said + statement` -> `said (that) + reported statement`**
- **`said to + person + statement` -> `told + person + (that) + reported statement`**

Also remember to check `pronouns`, `tense`, and `time expressions` where necessary. That is optional in many reported statements.

<br/>

We learned how to report statements using `said` and `said to`. <br/>
Now we'll learn when and how to use `told`, `explained`, and `replied` in indirect speech.

By the end of this topic, we'll be able to choose the correct reporting verb and transform sentences accurately.

### 1. Using `told`

We generally use `told` when we report information that someone communicated to another person.

Structure 

**Direct speech: `Subject + said to + person + "statement"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + told + person + (that) + reported statement`**

Examples:
|      Direct speech	                          |       Indirect speech                     |
|-----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Ravi said to me, "I am busy."	                | Ravi told me that he was busy.            |
| Neha said to Amit, "You are late."	          | Neha told Amit that he was late.          |
| The teacher said to us, "You have done well."	| The teacher told us that we had done well.|
| Mother said to Riya, "You should study."	    | Mother told Riya that she should study.   |

We can notice that `told` normally needs a person as its object.
- Correct: `She told me that she was tired.`
- Incorrect: `She told that she was tired.`
- Incorrect: `She told to me that she was tired.`
- Correct alternative: `She said that she was tired.`

Note: **Remember this pattern: `told + person`, not `told to + person`.**

### 2. Using `explained`

We use `explained` when someone clarifies an idea, gives a reason, or makes something easier to understand.

Structure 

**Direct speech: `Subject + said (to person) + "statement"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + explained + (to person) + (that) + statement`**

```
Direct:   The teacher said, "The Earth moves around the Sun."
Indirect: The teacher explained that the Earth moves around the Sun.
- The scientific fact remains true, so the tense need not change.

Direct:   Ravi said to me, "The road is closed because of repairs."
Indirect: Ravi explained to me that the road was closed because of repairs.
- The reporting verb indicates that Ravi was giving an explanation.

Direct:   The boy said, "I could not attend school because I was ill."
Indirect: The boy explained that he could not attend school because he had been ill.
- This is a possible backshifted version; the exact tense depends on the intended timeline.
```

Note: **Don't change the reporting verb mechanically. Use explained when the context shows that someone is clarifying something.**

### 3. Using `replied`

We use `replied` when someone answers what another person has said or asked.

Structure 

**Direct speech: `Subject + said (to person) + "statement"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + replied + (to person) + (that) + statement`**

Examples:
| Direct speech	                                 | Indirect speech                                    |
|------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Amit said, "I am ready."                       | Amit replied that he was ready.                    |
| Sita said to me, "I cannot help you."	         | Sita replied to me that she could not help me.     |
| The student said, "I have completed the work." | The student replied that he had completed the work.|

In everyday English, **`replied that`** and **`replied to someone that`** are both possible, though **`replied to someone that...`** can sound more formal. We can also use `replied to someone without that` when the sentence structure allows it.
```
The student said that he was ready.    - Simply reports his words.
The student replied that he was ready. - Shows that he was answering someone.

- The main difference is the context, not just the grammar.
```

### 4. Compare the three reporting verbs: `told`, `explained`, `replied`

| Reporting verb | When to use it	                | Example                                        |
|----------------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| `told`	       | Giving information to someone	| She told me that she was busy.                 |
| `explained`    | Clarifying an idea or reason	  | He explained that the machine was not working. |
| `replied`      | Answering someone	            | She replied that she was ready.                |

Remember: `told` usually takes a person as its object. With `explained` and `replied`, you can often use `to + person` when you mention the listener.

- `replied`: He replied that he was ready.
- Pronouns and tenses may change according to the speaker, listener, and reporting context.
- Choose a reporting verb that matches the meaning, not just the sentence pattern.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 8. When tense changes are required and when they are not </b> </summary>

<br/>

This is one of the most important things in Direct and Indirect Speech.

**The key principle**: 
> Tense changes depend on the reporting verb, whether the information is still true, and the time relationship between the original statement and the report.

### 1. Case A: The reporting verb is in the present or future

When the reporting verb is **in the present or future, we generally do not backshift the tense**.

Present Reporting Verb
```
Direct: Ravi says, “I am tired.”
Indirect: Ravi says that he is tired.
```

Future Reporting Verb
```
Direct: Ravi will say, “I am tired.”
Indirect: Ravi will say that he is tired.
```

**Why?** <br/> 
The reporting verb `says` or `will say` does not normally require the tense to move into the past.

Note: **Remember that pronouns can still change even when the tense remains the same.**


### 2. Case B: The reporting verb is in the past

When the reporting verb is **in the past, such as `said`, `told`, or `explained`, we generally backshift the tense**.

| Direct speech	      | Indirect speech                  |
|---------------------|----------------------------------|
| "I work hard."      |	He said that he worked hard.     |
| "I am working."     |	He said that he was working.     |
| "I have finished."  |	He said that he had finished.    |
| "I saw a bird."     |	He said that he had seen a bird. |
| "I will help you."	| He said that he would help me.   |

This is the standard pattern you should learn for conventional transformation questions. <br/>
However, backshift is not compulsory in every real-life situation.

### 3. Case C: Universal truths and scientific facts

For **a universal truth or scientific fact, we usually keep the tense in the present, even if the reporting verb is in the past**.

```
Example 1 - Universal truth
Direct:   The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
Indirect: The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.

Example 2 - Scientific fact
Direct:   The scientist said, "Water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure."
Indirect: The scientist said that water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.

Example 3 - General truth
Direct:   My father said, "Honesty is the best policy."
Indirect: My father said that honesty is the best policy.
```

Tip: Ask, "Is this still generally true?" If yes, retaining the present tense is usually appropriate.

In some textbook exercises, a past tense may also be accepted when the speaker is simply reporting what someone said, but keeping the present is the usual choice for enduring truths.

### 4. Case D: The situation is still true

Even if the reporting verb is in the past, we may keep the original tense when the statement remains true and the reporter wants to emphasize that it is still true.

Examples: 

Backshifted version
```
Direct:   Meena said, "I live in Delhi."
Indirect: Meena said that she lived in Delhi.
- This reports her statement from a past viewpoint.
- It does not necessarily tell us whether she still lives there.
```
Present-tense version
```
Direct:   Meena said, "I live in Delhi."
Indirect: Meena said that she lives in Delhi.
- This suggests that the information is still true now.
```

Both versions can be correct. <br/> 
The context and intended meaning determine which is more suitable.

### 5. Case E: Past perfect and modal verbs

Some verbs do not normally change further in indirect speech.

| Direct speech	    | Indirect speech	                | Rule                                         |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| "I had eaten."	  | He said that he had eaten.	    | Past perfect usually stays the same.         |
| "I could swim."	  | She said that she could swim.	  | `Could` usually stays the same.              |
| "I should study." | He said that he should study.	  | `Should` usually stays the same.             |
| "I might come."   |	She said that she might come.	  | `Might` usually stays the same.              |
| "I can swim."     |	He said that he could swim.	    | `Can commonly changes to `could`.            |
| "I must leave."   |	She said that she had to leave.	| `Must` may change to `had to` for obligation.|

Note that `must` can remain unchanged when the obligation is still relevant or the context favors it. <br/>
Modal changes depend on meaning, not just memorization.

### 6. A simple decision method for whether tense changes or not?

| Ask these questions in order. |
|-------------------------------|

> **1. Is the reporting verb in the present or future?** <br/>
Usually keep the original tense.   

> **2. Is the reporting verb in the past?** <br/>
Usually backshift the tense.      

> **3. Is it a universal truth or is the information still true?** <br/>
The present tense may be retained. 

> **4. Is the tense already past perfect, or is the verb a modal that usually stays unchanged?** <br/>
Check the standard tense and modal rules. 


### 7. Practice quiz

```
1. Riya says, "I am happy."
- Riya says that she is happy.
- Says is present tense, so the reported verb normally stays in the present.

2. Mohan said, "I play cricket."
- Mohan said that he played cricket.
- With a past reporting verb, the present simple normally backshifts to past simple.

3. The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
- The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
- A universal scientific truth normally remains in the present tense.

4. She said, "I have completed the work."
- She said that she had completed the work.
- Present perfect usually backshifts to past perfect after a past reporting verb.

5. Which sentence can retain the present tense because the information is still true?
a. He said that he lives in Mumbai, and he still lives there.
b. He said that he had lived there, but he moved away.
c. He said that he would travel the following day.

- He said that he lives in Mumbai, and he still lives there.
- The present tense can be retained to show that the information remains true.
```

### 8. Final revision

- Present/future reporting verb: Usually no tense backshift.
- Past reporting verb: Usually backshift the tense.
- Universal truths: Usually keep the present tense.
- Information still true: Present tense may be retained.
- Past perfect and certain modal verbs: Usually remain unchanged.

Important Note: Tense changes are rules with context-dependent exceptions, not automatic word substitutions.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 9. Negative statements and sentences with multiple clauses </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll learn how to change negative statements and longer sentences into indirect speech while keeping their meaning intact.

Sentences containing `not, never, no one, nothing, because, although, if, and that`.

### 1. Reporting negative statements

> A negative statement expresses that **something is not true, does not happen, or did not happen**.

Common negative words include:
- `not`: I am not ready.
- `never`: I have never visited Delhi.
- `no one`: No one helped me.
- `nothing`: I saw nothing.
- `neither…nor`: Neither Ravi nor Amit attended the meeting.

**The basic rule**
Keep the negative meaning when changing direct speech into indirect speech. Apply the usual pronoun and tense rules.

| Direct speech	                               | Indirect speech                                  |
|----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| He said, "I am not tired."                   |	He said that he was not tired.                  |
| Riya said, "I do not like coffee."           | Riya said that she did not like coffee.          |
| Aman said, "I have never seen a lion."       | Aman said that he had never seen a lion.         |
| She said, "Nobody helped me."                | She said that nobody had helped her.             |
| Rahul said, "I will not attend the meeting." | Rahul said that he would not attend the meeting. |

Note: **Do not remove the negative word when changing the tense.** <br/>
Example:
```
Direct:   He said, "I don't understand."
Indirect: He said that he didn't understand.

- The negative remains; the tense and pronoun change as required.
```

### 2. Negative statements with `said to` and `told`

Remember the rule: when reporting a statement addressed to a person, `said to + person` commonly becomes `told + person`.

Examples:
```
Direct:   Neha said to me, "I do not trust him."
Indirect: Neha told me that she did not trust him.

Direct:   The teacher said to the students, "You must not make noise."
Indirect: The teacher told the students that they must not make noise.

Must not expresses prohibition.
It can remain unchanged when the meaning is preserved.
```
Notice that `must not` does not automatically become `did not have to`. <br/>
These expressions have different meanings:
- `Must not`: something is prohibited.
- `Do not have to`: something is not necessary.
Example:
```
"You must not enter"        means entry is forbidden,
while
"You do not have to enter"  means entering is optional.
```

### 3. Sentences containing `because`, `although`, and `if`

> **A sentence can contain more than one clause.**

> **A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a verb.**

Example:
```
"I stayed at home because I was sick."
- Main clause: I stayed at home.
- Dependent clause: because I was sick.

When reporting a sentence with multiple clauses, preserve the relationship between the ideas.
Apply tense changes according to the context.
```

A. **Because - reason**
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "I did not attend school because I was ill."
Indirect: Ravi said that he had not attended school because he had been ill.
- The reason for not attending is preserved.
- Depending on the timeline, "was ill" may also remain unchanged.
```

B. **Although - contrast**
```
Direct:   Meena said, "Although I am tired, I will finish my work."
Indirect: Meena said that although she was tired, she would finish her work.
- Although still introduces the contrast between being tired and finishing the work.
```

C. **If - condition**
```
Direct:   Amit said, "If it rains, I will stay at home."
Indirect: Amit said that if it rained, he would stay at home.
- This is a common school-style transformation.
- The exact tense can depend on whether the condition is still possible or refers to a different time.
```

Tip: We should not change every verb mechanically. First understand the timeline, then apply the appropriate tense rules.

### 4. Sentences with `that` inside the quotation

Sometimes the word that is already part of the original sentence. We as a student may confuse it with the that used to introduce reported speech.
```
Direct:   Priya said, "I know that he is honest."
Indirect: Priya said that she knew that he was honest.

Here, the two instances of that have different roles:
- The first that introduces the reported statement.
- The second that introduces the clause he was honest.

You may omit the first "that" in many cases:
Indirect: Priya said she knew that he was honest.

Both forms are grammatically acceptable.
```

### 5. Sentences containing `never`, `nobody`, and `nothing`

Pay attention to negative meaning. Avoid accidentally creating a double negative.

| Direct speech	                        | Indirect speech                            |
|---------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| He said, "I never tell lies."	        | He said that he never told lies.           |
| She said, "Nobody called me."	        | She said that nobody had called her.       |
| Ravi said, "I found nothing."	        | Ravi said that he had found nothing.       |
| The boy said, "I cannot do anything."	| The boy said that he could not do anything.|

The main goal is to preserve the original meaning while changing the grammatical perspective.

### 6. Common mistakes

Mistake 1: Removing the negative
```
Original:  He said, "I am not tired."
Incorrect: He said that he was tired.
Correct:   He said that he was not tired.
```
Mistake 2: Changing the meaning of a prohibition
```
Original:  The teacher said, "You must not enter."
Incorrect: The teacher told us that we did not have to enter.
Better:    The teacher told us that we must not enter.
```
Mistake 3: Forgetting the pronoun change
```
Direct:   Sita said, "I will not help you."
Indirect: Sita said that she would not help me.
- This assumes the reporter is the person Sita addressed as "you".
```

### 7. Practice quiz to test your understanding

```
1. Rahul said, "I do not eat meat."
- Rahul told that he did not eat meat.
- I changes to he, and do not eat usually changes to did not eat.

2. She said, "I have never visited Jaipur."
- She told that she had never visited Jaipur.
- Present perfect usually backshifts to past perfect. Never remains.

3. The teacher said to us, "You must not run in the corridor."
- The teacher told us that we must not run in the corridor.
- Must not expresses prohibition. Told takes the object us.

4. Meena said, "Although I am busy, I will help you."
- Meena told that although she was busy, she would help me.
- Assuming I am the person Meena addressed, the pronouns change. Although preserves the contrast.

5. Priya said, "I know that he is honest."
- Priya told that she knew that he was honest
- Both clauses are reported. The first that introduces the report; the second belongs to the original sentence.
```

### 8. Lesson recap

- Keep the negative meaning of the original sentence.
- Change pronouns and tenses according to the speaker, listener, and context.
- Preserve conjunctions such as `because`, `although`, and `if` when they express the original relationship between ideas.
- Distinguish `must not` (prohibition) from `do not have to` (lack of necessity).
- A sentence may contain multiple clauses, each of which must retain its intended meaning.

  <summary> <b> 10. Yes/No Questions using <code> if </code> and <code> whether </code> </b> </summary>

<br/>

So far, We've learned to report statements. <br/>
Now we'll learn how to report questions in indirect speech.

> **Yes/No Questions - questions that can be answered with yes or no.**

Examples:
```
Q: Are you happy?
- Yes/No
Q: Do you play cricket?
- Yes/No
Q: Have you finished your homework?
- Yes/No
Q: Will you come tomorrow?
- Yes/No
```
The most important rule is this: <br/>
When reporting a **Yes/No Question**, use `if` or `whether`, and change the question into statement word order.

### 1. What is a Yes/No question?

A Yes/No question asks whether something is true or whether something happens.

```
Direct question:   Ravi said to me, "Are you ready?"
Indirect question: Ravi asked me if I was ready.
```
Notice three changes:
1. Reporting verb: `said to` becomes `asked`.
2. Connecting word: `if` is added instead of `that`.
3. Word order: `"Are you ready?"` becomes `"I was ready"` - subject before verb.

We also change `you` to `I` because we're assuming the reporter is the person Ravi addressed.

### 2. The basic formula

**Direct speech: `Subject + asked + person + "auxiliary verb + subject + ...?"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + asked + person + if/whether + subject + verb ...`**

Examples:
| Direct speech	                       | Indirect speech                        |
|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "Are you busy?"       | He asked me if I was busy.             |
| She said to him, "Do you like tea?"  | She asked him whether he liked tea.    |
| Mother said to me, "Have you eaten?" | Mother asked me if I had eaten.        |
| Ravi said to Sita, "Will you come?"  | Ravi asked Sita whether she would come.|

We can often use `if` or `whether` interchangeably in these sentences.

### 3. Why does the word order change?

This is one of the most important concepts in reported questions.

In a direct question, the auxiliary verb often comes before the subject.
```
Direct:   "Are you happy?"
Reported: He asked me if I was happy.

Direct:   "Can you swim?"
Reported: He asked me whether I could swim.

Direct:   "Have you finished?"
Reported: She asked me if I had finished.
```
Notice that the reported question follows **statement word order: subject first, then verb**.
```
Direct:    She said to me, "Are you ready?"
Incorrect: She asked me if was I ready.
Correct:   She asked me if I was ready.
```

### 4. Questions with `do`, `does`, and `did`

In direct questions, `do`, `does`, and `did` often help form the question. <br/>
In indirect speech, these auxiliary verbs usually disappear as separate question helpers, and the main verb takes the appropriate tense.

Example 1: Do
```
Direct: Amit said to me, "Do you play football?"
Indirect: Amit asked me if I played football.

Step by step:
- said to me -> asked me
- Add if or whether.
- you play -> I played (assuming the reporter is the person addressed).
```
Example 2: `Does`
```
Direct:   Riya said to him, "Does your brother work here?"
Indirect: Riya asked him whether his brother worked there.
```
Example 3: `Did`
```
Direct:   The teacher said to Ravi, "Did you finish the task?"
Indirect: The teacher asked Ravi if he had finished the task.
```
In traditional school exercises, `did you finish` commonly becomes `he had finished.` Depending on context, `he finished` can also be acceptable.


### 5. When should we use `if` versus `whether`?

Both `if` and `whether` are usually correct in ordinary Yes/No reported questions.
```
She asked me if I was hungry.
She asked me whether I was hungry.
```

Use `whether` especially in more formal contexts, before or not, or when the sentence presents alternatives. <br/>
Examples:
```
He asked whether or not I agreed.
She asked whether I wanted tea or coffee.
```
For now, remember: **`if/whether + subject + verb`**

### 6. Practice quiz

```
1. He said to me, "Are you tired?"
- He asked me if I was tired.
- Use asked + person + if + subject + verb.

2. She said to Ravi, "Do you speak English?"
- She asked Ravi whether he spoke English.
- Do disappears as a question helper, and speak changes to spoke in the standard backshifted form.

3. Mother said to me, "Have you finished your homework?"
- Mother asked me if I had finished my homework.
- Have you finished becomes I had finished, with statement word order.

4. The teacher said to the boy, "Did you break the window?"
- The teacher asked the boy if he had broken the window.
- Did you break commonly backshifts to he had broken.

5. Choose the correct sentence.
a. She asked whether was I ready.
b. She asked whether I was ready.
c. She asked that whether I was ready
- She asked whether I was ready.
- Reported questions use statement word order.
-  Do not add that before whether.
```

### 7. Quick Recap

1. Yes/No questions generally use `asked + if/whether`.
2. Change `said to` to `asked` when reporting a question.
3. Use statement word order: `"if I was ready,"` not `"if was I ready."`
4. Change pronouns and tenses according to the context.

  <summary> <b> 11. WH-questions: <code> what, why, where, when, who, and how </code> </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll learn how to report questions that begin with `what, where, when, why, who, whom, which, and how`.
These are called **WH-questions** (although how doesn't begin with WH).

### 1. What is a WH-question?

A WH-question asks for specific information rather than a simple yes or no.

Examples:
```
What is your name?                [What] 
Asks about a thing or information.

Where do you live?                [Where]
Asks about a place.

When will you arrive?             [When]
Asks about time.

Why are you crying?               [Why]
Asks about a reason.

How did you solve it?             [How]
Asks about a method or manner.
```

### 2. The main rule

Keep the original WH-word, change the reporting verb as needed, and use statement word order.

The general structure is:
**Direct speech: `Subject + asked + person + "WH-word + auxiliary verb + subject + ...?"`** <br/>
**Indirect speech: `Subject + asked + person + WH-word + subject + verb ...`**

Unlike **Yes/No questions**, **WH-questions** generally do not need `if` or `whether`. <br/>
Example
```
Direct:   Ravi said to me, "Where do you live?"
Indirect: Ravi asked me where I lived.

Step 1: Change said to me to asked me.
Step 2: Keep where.
Step 3: Change do you live to I lived, assuming the reporter is the person addressed.

Notice that we do not say "where did I live" in this reported question.
```

### 3. Examples of all major WH-words

| Direct speech	                                    | Indirect speech                              |
|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "What do you want?"	              | He asked me what I wanted.                   |
| She said to him, "Where are you going?"	          | She asked him where he was going.            |
| Ravi said to me, "When will you return?"	        | Ravi asked me when I would return.           |
| Mother said to her, "Why are you crying?"	        | Mother asked her why she was crying.         |
| The teacher said to Ravi, "How did you solve it?"	| The teacher asked Ravi how he had solved it. |
| She said to me, "Which book do you prefer?"	      | She asked me which book I preferred.         |
| He said to me, "Who is your teacher?"	            | He asked me who my teacher was.              |

The pronouns depend on who was speaking and who was being addressed. <br/. 
The examples above assume the identities are clear from the context.

### 4. Questions with `do`, `does`, and `did`

This is an important rule because students often leave `do`, `does`, or `did` in the reported sentence.

In ordinary direct questions, these words help form the question. <br/>
When reporting it, they usually disappear as separate question helpers.
```
Direct: He asked me, "Where do you work?"                [Do]
Indirect: He asked me where I worked.

Direct: She asked him, "Where does your brother study?"  [Does]
Indirect: She asked him where his brother studied.

Direct: The teacher asked me, "Why did you leave early?" [Did]
Indirect: The teacher asked me why I had left early.
```
For standard school transformations, the past simple commonly backshifts to past perfect. 
In real usage, the past simple may also be retained when the context supports it.

### 5. Questions that begin with `who`

Be careful with `who`, because it can be the subject or the object of a question.

**When `who` is the subject**
```
Direct:   She asked me, "Who broke the window?"
Indirect: She asked me who had broken the window.
- Here, who performs the action. We don't need to add another subject after it.
```

**When `who` is the object**
```
Direct:   She asked me, "Who did you invite?"
Indirect: She asked me who I had invited.
- Here, you is the subject, and who is the object.
- In indirect speech, use the subject before the verb.
```
Tip: Ask, **"Who is doing the action?"** This helps them identify the correct sentence structure.


### 6. Common mistakes

```
Wrong:   He asked me where did I live.
Correct: He asked me where I lived.
- Use statement word order.

Wrong:   She asked me that why I was late.
Correct: She asked me why I was late.
- Do not normally add that before the WH-word.

Wrong:   He asked me if where I lived.
Correct: He asked me where I lived.
- WH-questions retain their WH-word and generally don't use if or whether.
```

### 7. Practice quiz

```
1. She said to me, "Where do you live?"
- She asked me where I lived.
- Keep where and use statement word order: I lived.

2. He said to Ravi, "Why are you laughing?"
- He asked Ravi why he was laughing.
- The WH-word remains, and the subject comes before the verb.

3. The teacher said to me, "How did you solve this problem?"
- The teacher asked me how I had solved that problem.
- Did you solve commonly becomes I had solved in the standard backshifted form;
- this may change to that depending on context.

4. She asked me, "Who broke the glass?"
- She asked me who had broken the glass.
- Who is the subject of broke, so no extra subject is needed.

5. He said to her, "Which dress will you wear?"
- He asked her which dress she would wear.
- Keep which dress and use statement word order: she would wear.
```

### 8. Quick Revision

Remember these four points:
1. Keep the **WH-word: `what, where, when, why, who, which, or how`**.
2. Change `said to` to `asked` when reporting a question.
3. Use statement word order: `WH-word + subject + verb`.
4. Remove `do, does, and did` as separate question helpers, then adjust the main verb as needed.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 12. Word order in indirect questions </b> </summary>

<br/>

This is extremely important because we often know how to change the tense and pronouns but still make mistakes with word order.
```
Direct:   He said to me, "Where are you going?"
Indirect: He asked me where was I going.       [Incorrect]
Indirect: He asked me where I was going.       [Correct]
- The words are almost the same, but the grammar is different.
```

### 1. The golden rule

**In an indirect question, use statement word order: `subject + verb`.**

Examples:
```
DIRECT QUESTION:   Where are you going?
INDIRECT QUESTION: He asked me where I was going.
```

| Direct question	       | Indirect question               |
|------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Where are you?	       | He asked where you were.        |
| What is she doing?	   | He asked what she was doing.    |
| Why did he leave?	     | She asked why he had left.      |
| When will they arrive? | He asked when they would arrive.|
| Where can I sit?	     | She asked where she could sit.  |

In direct questions, the auxiliary verb often comes before the subject. <br/>
In indirect questions, the subject normally comes before the verb.

### 2. Yes/No questions also use statement word order

For Yes/No questions, use **`if` or `whether`, followed by the subject and verb**.

Examples:
```
Direct:   "Are you ready?"
Indirect: She asked me if I was ready.
Indirect: She asked me if was I ready.  [Not]

Direct:   "Can you swim?"
Indirect: He asked me whether I could swim.
Indirect: He asked me whether could I swim. [Not]
```

The structure is: <br/>
**Yes/No question: `Reporting verb + if/whether + subject + verb`** <br/>
**WH-question: `Reporting verb + WH-word + subject + verb`**

### 3. What about questions with `do`, `does`, and `did`?

These words `do`, `does`, and `did` frequently cause errors.

When they are used as question helpers, they usually disappear in the reported question, and the main verb takes the appropriate form.

Examples
```
Do
Direct:   "Where do you work?"
Indirect: He asked me where I worked.
- Do disappears; work becomes worked in the usual backshifted form.

Does
Direct:   "Where does she live?"
Indirect: He asked where she lived.
- Does disappears; the main verb changes to lived.

Did
Direct:   "Why did he leave?"
Indirect: She asked why he had left.
- In the standard school transformation, did leave becomes had left.
```

Important distinction: do can also be the main verb, not just a question helper.
```
Direct:   "What do you do?"
Indirect: He asked me what I did.
- Here, the first do is a question helper;
- the second do carries the meaning of performing work or an activity.
```

### 4. Polite indirect questions

Now let's learn something useful beyond traditional exam transformations.
In everyday English, we often use polite expressions such as:
```
Could you tell me…?
Do you know…?
Can you explain…?
Would you mind telling me…?
```
When a question is embedded after one of these expressions, it follows **statement word order**.

| Direct question	              | Polite indirect question                      |
|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Where is the station?	        | Could you tell me where the station is?       |
| What time does the shop open?	| Do you know what time the shop opens?         |
| Where did she go?	            | Can you tell me where she went?               |
| Is he at home?	              | Do you know if he is at home?                 |
| When will the train arrive?	  | Could you tell me when the train will arrive? |

Notice something important: in the polite indirect question, the embedded clause does not automatically backshift just because the opening expression contains `could` or `did`.

Example:
```
Direct: "Where is the station?"
Polite: "Could you tell me where the station is?"
- We keep is because the question is about the station's current location.
```

### 5. The difference between reported and polite questions

These two forms use the same word-order principle, but they serve different purposes.

Reported question
```
Direct:   He said to me, "Where is the bank?"
Reported: He asked me where the bank was.
- This reports a question someone asked.
- The past reporting verb commonly causes backshift.
```
Polite indirect question
```
Direct question: "Where is the bank?"
Polite form:     Could you tell me where the bank is?
- This is a question you ask directly and politely.
- The bank's current location remains in the present tense.
```

### 6. Common mistakes

```
Incorrect: Could you tell me where is the library?
Correct:   Could you tell me where the library is?

Incorrect: She asked me why did I cry.
Correct:   She asked me why I cried.
- A backshifted version could be "why I had cried," depending on the intended timeline.

Incorrect: Do you know where does he live?
Correct:   Do you know where he lives?
```

```

-  Use statement word order: I was going.

2. Choose the correct polite question.
a. Could you tell me where is the hospital?
b. Could you tell me where the hospital is?
c. Could you tell me where does the hospital be?
-  Could you tell me where the hospital is?
-  The embedded question uses subject + verb: the hospital is.

3. He asked me, "Why did you leave?"
-  He asked me why I had left.
-  Standard transformation uses statement word order, commonly backshifts to past perfect.

4. Choose the correct sentence.
a. Do you know what time does the bus arrive?
b. Do you know what time the bus arrives?

5. Which sentence is correct?
a. He asked whether was she busy.

```

### 8. Quick Final revision

Remember these rules:
1. **Indirect questions** normally use **statement word order**.
2. **WH-questions** retain the **WH-word**.
3. **Yes/No questions** use **`if` or `whether`**.
4. **`Do`, `does`, and `did`** generally disappear as question helpers.
5. Polite indirect questions, such as `"Could you tell me where the station is?"`, also use **statement word order**.

  <summary> <b> 13. Questions with do, does, and did </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll focus on one of the most common sources of mistakes in Direct and Indirect Speech: questions beginning with `do`, `does`, and `did`.

The key rule is that these `do`, `does`, and `did` words usually disappear when they function as question helpers. <br/> The main verb then takes the appropriate form, and the reported question uses statement word order.

### 1. Understand the role of `do`, `does`, and `did`

First, understand how these words work in direct questions.
| Direct question	        | What it tells us              |
|-------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Do you play cricket?	  | Present simple; subject `you` |
| Does she play cricket?	| Present simple; subject `she` |
| Did he play cricket?	  | Past simple; subject `he`     |

Notice that the main verb is `play` in all three questions. <br/>
We use `do`, `does`, or `did` to form the question.

When we report these questions, the question helper usually disappears. 

### 2. Questions with `do`

Rule <br/>
**Direct: `Do + subject + base verb ...?`** <br/>
**Indirect: `asked + if/whether + subject + verb ...`**

Example:
```
Direct:   Rahul said to me, "Do you play football?"
Indirect: Rahul asked me if I played football.

Step by step:
1. "said to" me becomes "asked" me.
2. Add "if" or "whether".
3. Change "you" to "I", assuming I am the person Rahul addressed.
4. Change "play" to "played" in the usual backshifted form.
```

| Direct speech	                               | Indirect speech                            |
|----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "Do you like tea?"	           | He asked me if I liked tea.                |
| She said to Ravi, "Do you know the answer?"	 | She asked Ravi whether he knew the answer. |
| The teacher said to us, "Do you understand?" | The teacher asked us if we understood.     |

### 3. Questions with `does`

Rule <br/>
**Direct: `Does + subject + base verb ...?`**
When changing to indirect speech, does disappears as the question helper. The main verb takes the appropriate form.

Example:
```
Direct:   Riya said to me, "Does your brother work here?"
Indirect: Riya asked me whether my brother worked there.

Step by step:
- Said to me → asked me
- Keep the question's meaning.
- Does your brother work → my brother worked, assuming Riya addressed the person reporting.
- Here may become there if the reporting viewpoint is different.
```

| Direct speech	                                | Indirect speech                                |
|-----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------| 
| He said to her, "Does your father drive?"	    | He asked her if her father drove.              |
| The teacher said, "Does everyone understand?"	| The teacher asked whether everyone understood. |
| She said to him, "Does this bus go to Delhi?"	| She asked him if that bus went to Delhi.       |

The words this and here change only when the context calls for it.

### 4. Questions with `did`

This is especially important because we sometimes keep `did` in the indirect question.

Rule <br/>
**Direct: `Did + subject + base verb ...?`** <br/>

In standard school transformations, the **past simple often changes to the past perfect**.

Example:
```
Direct:   The teacher said to Ravi, "Did you complete the homework?"
Indirect: The teacher asked Ravi whether he had completed the homework.

Step by step:
1. Said to Ravi becomes asked Ravi.
2. Remove did as the question helper.
3. Change you to he.
4. Change complete to had completed.
```

| Direct speech	                                      | Indirect speech                                  |
|-----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "Did you see the movie?"	            | He asked me if I had seen the movie.             |
| She said to him, "Did you call your mother?"	      | She asked him whether he had called his mother.  |
| The teacher said to me, "Did you write the answer?"	| The teacher asked me if I had written the answer.|

Depending on context, the past simple can sometimes remain unchanged in natural English. <br/>
For school exercises, learn the standard backshifted form first.

### 5. Exception: when `do` is the main verb

In some sentences, do is not merely a question helper; it is the main verb meaning perform or carry out.
```
Direct:   He said to me, "What do you do?"
Indirect: He asked me what I did.
Here:
- The first "do" is the question helper and disappears.
- The second "do" is the main verb and remains, changing to "did".

Direct:   She said to him, "Did you do your homework?"
Indirect: She asked him whether he had done his homework.
- The main verb "do" changes to "done" as part of the past perfect construction.
```

### 6. Common mistakes

```
Incorrect: He asked me if did I play cricket.
Correct:   He asked me if I played cricket.
- Use statement word order.

Incorrect: She asked me does my brother work there.
Correct:   She asked me if my brother worked there.
- Do not keep does as a question helper in the reported clause.

Incorrect: He asked me if I had saw the film.
Correct:   He asked me if I had seen the film.
- Past perfect requires had + past participle: had seen, had written, had gone.
```

2. She said to Ravi, "Does your sister study English?"
-  She asked Ravi whether his sister studied English.
-  "does" disappears; the subject comes before the verb, and study changes to studied.

3. The teacher said to me, "Did you finish the test?"
-  The teacher asked me if I had finished the test.
-  In the standard school transformation, "did" finish becomes "had" finished.

### 8. Quick Revision 

Remember these rules:

1. **`Do/does`**: Remove the question helper and adjust the main verb.
2. **`Did`**: Remove the question helper; in standard school transformations, use the past perfect where appropriate.
3. Use **`asked + if/whether`** for **Yes/No questions**.
4. Use **statement word order**: **subject before verb**.
5. If `do` is the main verb, do not remove its meaning.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 14. Negative questions and question tags in reported speech </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll learn how to report questions such as:
```
"Don't you know the answer?"
"Isn't she coming?"
"You are ready, aren't you?"
"He didn't call you, did he?"
```
The key is to **report the meaning of the question, use statement word order, and choose a suitable reporting verb**.

### 1. Negative questions

A negative question contains a negative word such as `not` or a contraction such as `don't, isn't, didn't, or can't`.

Examples:
```
"Don't you understand?"
"Isn't he at home?"
"Didn't she finish her work?"
```
When changing these into indirect speech:
1. Use a reporting verb such as `asked` or `wanted to know`.
2. Use `if` or `whether` for a **yes/no question**.
3. Change the **question order** to **statement order**: `subject + verb`.
4. Preserve the negative meaning when it is part of the question.

Examples
```
Direct speech:   "Don't you know the answer?" the teacher said to Ravi.
Indirect speech: The teacher asked Ravi if he didn't know the answer.

Direct speech:   "Isn't she coming today?" Meena asked.
Indirect speech: Meena asked whether she wasn't coming that day.

Direct speech:  "Didn't you call me?" Rahul asked me.
Indirect speech: Rahul asked me if I hadn't called him.
```

Important: These examples show the standard school-style transformation, including tense backshift. 

In natural English, negative questions can express surprise, expectation, or disappointment, so the best wording sometimes depends on the speaker's intended meaning. 

Example: `"Didn't you call me?"` may also be reported as `"Rahul asked why I hadn't called him,"` if the context is about the reason.


### 2. What are question tags?

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement to seek confirmation or agreement.

|   Statement      | Question tag  |
|------------------|---------------|
| "You are ready"  | "aren't you?" |
| "She can swim"   | "can't she?"  |
| "He didn't come" | "did he?"     |

A tag usually has the opposite polarity from the **statement: `positive statement + negative tag`, or `negative statement + positive tag`**.


### 3. How do we report question tags?

In reported speech, we normally report the main statement and its purpose, rather than preserving the little tag as a separate question.

For a tag seeking confirmation, common reporting verbs include `asked`, `wanted to know`, and `checked`.

| Direct speech	                                      | Reported speech                            |
|-----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "You are tired, aren't you?"	        | He asked me if I was tired.                |
| She said, "Ravi has left, hasn't he?"               | She asked whether Ravi had left.           |
| Aman said to me, "You didn't forget, did you?"	    | Aman asked me if I hadn't forgotten.       |
| The teacher said, "You have finished, haven't you?"	| The teacher asked whether we had finished. |

Notice that **the tag itself disappears, and we use `if/whether + subject + verb`**.

### 4. When the speaker is expecting agreement

Sometimes a tag is more like a request for agreement than a genuine question.
```
Possible indirect versions:

Direct: "It's a beautiful day, isn't it?" she said.
Indirect: She remarked that it was a beautiful day.
Indirect: She said that it was a beautiful day and asked whether I agreed.
```
The first version is suitable when the speaker is mainly making a remark. <br/> 
The second makes the request for agreement explicit. <br/>
Context determines the most natural choice.

### 5. Common mistakes to avoid

```
Incorrect: He asked me if was I tired.
Correct:   He asked me if I was tired.
- Use statement word order after if/whether.

Incorrect:       She asked, "You are ready, aren't you?"
Indirect speech: She asked that I was ready, wasn't I?
Better:          She asked me whether I was ready.
- Usually remove the tag and report the question's meaning.
```

Remember: **A negative question does not always mean the speaker expects a negative answer.** <br/>
`"Don't you like mangoes?"` may express surprise that someone might not like them.

### 6. Practice quiz

```
1. The teacher said to Rohan, "Don't you understand the lesson?"
-  The teached asked Rohan if he didn't understand the lesson.

### 1. What is an imperative sentence?

An imperative sentence expresses an order, command, instruction, or direction. <br/>
Its subject, **`you`**, is usually understood rather than stated.

Examples:
```
"Stand up!"
"Open the door."
"Complete your homework."
"Please sit down."
```

### 2. The main rule

Change the direct command into this structure: **`Subject + told + object + to + verb`**

| Direct speech	                                        | Indirect speech                                    |
|-------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "Stand up."	                          | He told me to stand up.                            |
| Mother said to Riya, "Clean your room."	              | Mother told Riya to clean her room.                |
| The teacher said to the students, "Read the chapter."	| The teacher told the students to read the chapter. |
| The officer said to the driver, "Stop the car."	      | The officer ordered the driver to stop the car.    |

Remember: We generally use told or ordered instead of said to when reporting a command. We do not normally use that before the infinitive.
```
Direct:    He said to me, "Stand up."
Incorrect: He told me that stand up.
Correct:   He told me to stand up.
```

### 3. How to transform a command step by step

```
Direct speech: The teacher said to Aman, "Write neatly.”\"
- 1. Identify the reporting verb: "said to".
- 2. Recognize the sentence type: command/instruction.
- 3. Change "said to Aman" to "told Aman".
- 4. Change "write" to "to write".
Final answer: The teacher told Aman to write neatly.
```

### 4. Choose the reporting verb carefully

- **`told`** - an ordinary instruction or command.
- **`ordered`** - a strong or authoritative command.
- **`commanded`** - a forceful order, often from someone in authority.
- **`instructed`** - a formal or procedural direction.

The best choice depends on the situation and intended tone.

-  The teacher told Rahul to open his book.
-  Use told + object + to + base verb.
-  The possessive your changes according to the person addressed.

2. The captain said to the soldiers, "March forward!"
-  The captain ordered the soldiers to march forward.
-  Ordered suits a formal command, followed by object + to + verb.

3. Mother said to me, "Help your younger brother."
-  Mother told me to help my younger brother.
-  The listener becomes the object me, and your changes to my because the command is addressed to me.

### 1. What is a request?

A request is when we ask someone to do something politely rather than giving a direct order.

Examples:
```
"Please help me."
"Could you lend me your pen?"
"Kindly wait outside."
"Please don't make noise."
```

### 2. The main rule

Use this structure:

**Reported request pattern: `Subject + asked/requested + object + to + base verb`** <br/>
**For a negative request: `object + not to + base verb`**

| Direct speech	                                      | Indirect speech                              |
|-----------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "Please help me."	                  | He asked me to help him.                     |
| Mother said to Riya, "Please clean your room."	    | Mother asked Riya to clean her room.         |
| The man said to the guard, "Kindly open the gate."	| The man requested the guard to open the gate.|
| She said to me, "Please wait here."	                | She asked me to wait there.                  |

Notice that `please` and `kindly` are usually omitted in indirect speech because the reporting verb already expresses the request.

### 3. When should we use `asked` and `requested`?

`asked`: Used for ordinary, everyday requests.
```
Direct:   Ravi said to me, "Please lend me your book."
Indirect: Ravi asked me to lend him my book.
```
`requested`: Often used for formal or particularly polite requests.
```
Direct:   The principal said to the teacher, "Please attend the meeting."
Indirect: The principal requested the teacher to attend the meeting.
```
Both verbs can be correct in many situations. The choice depends on the context and tone.

### 4. Requests that use questions

A request may be expressed as a question.
```
Direct:   He said to me, "Could you open the door, please?"
Indirect: He asked me to open the door.

Direct:   She said to Rahul, "Would you help me?"
Indirect: She asked Rahul to help her.
```
Tip: Don't automatically report these as ordinary yes/no questions. 
If `"Could you…?"` or `"Would you…?"` is being used to make a request, the infinitive pattern is usually more natural.

```
"Can you swim?"            -> He asked me if I could swim.    (Question about ability)
"Can you open the window?" -> He asked me to open the window. (Request)
```
The meaning determines the pattern.

### 5. Negative requests

When the speaker asks someone not to do something, use: **`asked + object + not to + verb`**

Examples:
```
Direct:   Mother said to me, "Please don't go outside."
Indirect: Mother asked me not to go outside.

Direct:   The teacher said to the students, "Please don't talk."
Indirect: The teacher asked the students not to talk.

Direct:   He said to her, "Please don't tell anyone."
Indirect: He asked her not to tell anyone.
```
Notice that we do not say `asked me to not go` in the standard school pattern, although that word order can occur in natural English.

We learned **commands using `told/ordered + object + to + verb`**. <br/> 
We also learned **requests using `asked/requested + object + to + verb`.**

Now we'll learn how to report **advice, warnings, and instructions** by choosing the correct reporting verb.

### 1. Advice - use `advised`

`Advice` tells someone what they should or should not do.

**Structure: `Subject + advised + object + to + verb`**

Examples:
| Direct speech	                                       | Indirect speech                                     |
|------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| The doctor said to me, "Take rest."                  | The doctor advised me to take rest.                 |

| The teacher said to us, "Revise your lessons daily." | The teacher advised us to revise our lessons daily. |
| My friend said to me, "Don't waste your time."	     | My friend advised me not to waste my time.          |

Tip: For negative advice, use `advised + object + not to + verb`.

### 2. Warnings - use `warned`

A `warning` tells someone about a danger or possible harmful result.

**Structure: `Subject + warned + object + to/not to verb`**

Examples:
```
Direct speech:   The policeman said to the driver, "Don't drive so fast!"
Indirect speech: The policeman warned the driver not to drive so fast.

Direct speech:   Mother said to the child, "Don't touch the hot pan!"
Indirect speech: Mother warned the child not to touch the hot pan.

Direct speech:   The guard said to us, "Beware of the dog!"
Indirect speech: The guard warned us to beware of the dog.
```

A `warning` can also include the danger itself:
```
Direct:   The doctor said to him, "Don't smoke, or your health will suffer."
Indirect: The doctor warned him not to smoke, adding that his health would suffer.

- The exact wording may vary, provided the warning's meaning is preserved.
```

### 3. Instructions - use `instructed`

`Instructions` explain how to do something or what steps to follow. <br/>
They are often given by teachers, officials, supervisors, or people explaining a process.

Examples:
```
Direct:   The teacher said to the students, "Write your names on the answer sheets."
Indirect: The teacher instructed the students to write their names on the answer sheets.

Direct:   The manager said to the staff, "Submit the report by Friday."
Indirect: The manager instructed the staff to submit the report by Friday.

Direct:   The guide said to the visitors, "Follow me."
Indirect: The guide instructed the visitors to follow him.
```

### 4. Prohibitions - use `forbade` or `prohibited`

A `prohibition` means that something is not allowed.

**Structure: `Subject + prohibited/forbade + object + to + verb`**

Examples:
| Direct speech	                                    | Indirect speech                            |
|---------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| The teacher said to us, "Don't use calculators."	| The teacher forbade us to use calculators. |
| Father said to me, "Don't go out late."	          | Father forbade me to go out late.          |
| The sign said, "Do not enter."	                  | The sign prohibited entry.                 |

Important distinction:
- He **warned** me not to touch the wire.  -> He emphasized danger.
- He **forbade** me to touch the wire.     -> He prohibited the action.
- He **advised** me not to touch the wire. -> He recommended against it.
All three can refer to the same action, but their meanings and emphasis differ.

### 5. Compare the reporting verbs

| Purpose	    | Reporting verb	     | Example pattern        |
|-------------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Advice	    | `advised`            | advised me to study    |
| Warning	    | `warned`	           | warned me not to enter |
| Instruction	| `instructed`	       | instructed us to wait  |
| Prohibition	| `forbade/prohibited` | forbade us to enter    |
| Command	    | `ordered`	           | ordered him to stop    |
| Request	    | `asked`	             | asked me to help       |

Remember: choose the verb based on the speaker's intention, not just the punctuation.

Indirect: The teacher advised the students never to tell lies.

Direct:   Mother said to me, "Never touch that wire."
Indirect: Mother warned me never to touch that wire.

Both preserve the negative meaning. In school exercises, `never` to is often the most natural choice when the original command uses `never`.

Indirect: The teacher told the monitor not to let the students leave.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 19. Exclamatory sentences: joy, sorrow, surprise, and admiration </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll learn how to report **exclamatory sentences - sentences that express strong feelings or emotions**.

### 1. What is an exclamatory sentence?

An exclamatory sentence expresses a strong emotion. <br/>
It often ends with an exclamation mark (`!`).

Examples:
```
Joy:        "Hurrah! We have won the match!"
Sorrow:     "Alas! My friend has died!"
Surprise:   "What a surprise!"
Admiration: "How beautiful the garden is!"
Regret:     "Oh! I have lost my wallet!"
```
When changing these sentences into indirect speech, we generally remove the exclamation words and express the emotion through a reporting verb or a suitable statement.

### 2. Reporting joy - `exclaimed with joy`

When someone expresses happiness or success, use exclaimed with joy or exclaimed happily.

Examples:
```
Direct speech:   The players said, "Hurrah! We have won the match!"
Indirect speech: The players exclaimed with joy that they had won the match.

Direct speech:   Riya said, "What a wonderful gift this is!"
Indirect speech: Riya exclaimed with delight that it was a wonderful gift.
```
Notice that `Hurrah!` is omitted and the feeling of happiness is expressed by the reporting phrase.

### 3. Reporting sorrow - `exclaimed with sorrow`

When someone expresses sadness, grief, or disappointment, use exclaimed with sorrow or exclaimed sadly.

Examples:
```
Direct:   He said, "Alas! I have failed the examination!"
Indirect: He exclaimed with sorrow that he had failed the examination.

Direct:   She said, "Alas! My pet is dead!"
Indirect: She exclaimed with sorrow that her pet was dead.
```
The exact tense depends on the original sentence and context. <br/>
In standard school transformations, tense backshift is commonly applied after a past reporting verb.

### 4. Reporting surprise - `exclaimed with surprise`

Use this expression when the speaker is amazed or astonished.

Examples:
```
Direct:   She said, "What a huge building it is!"
Indirect: She exclaimed with surprise that it was a huge building.

Direct:   He said, "How clever the boy is!"
Indirect: He exclaimed with surprise that the boy was very clever.
```
In natural English, we can also say `"She was surprised that…"` or `"He exclaimed that…"`, depending on the intended meaning.

### 5. Reporting admiration - `exclaimed with admiration`

Admiration expresses appreciation for beauty, skill, or a good quality.

Examples:
```
Direct:   The visitors said, "How beautiful the painting is!"
Indirect: The visitors exclaimed with admiration that the painting was very beautiful.

Direct:   He said, "What a brilliant performance!"
Indirect: He exclaimed with admiration that it was a brilliant performance.
```

### 6. The transformation rules

Use these as a guide, not as a mechanical formula for every sentence.

| Direct speech expression	  | Common reported form                   |
|-----------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| `Hurrah!`	                  | `exclaimed with joy`                   |
| `Alas!`	                    | `exclaimed with sorrow`                |
| `What a…!`	                | `exclaimed that…`                      |
| `How beautiful…!`	          | `exclaimed that…`                      |
| `Oh! / What a surprise!`	  | `exclaimed with surprise`              |
| `What a wonderful…!`	      | `exclaimed with admiration or delight` |

Important grammar point <br/>
For exclamations beginning with `what` or `how`, change the exclamatory word order into statement order.
```
Direct:   She said, "How beautiful the flowers are!"
Indirect: She exclaimed that the flowers were very beautiful.
Not:      She exclaimed how beautiful were the flowers.
```
However, if the original sentence is genuinely an information question rather than an exclamation, the structure may differ. Always identify the sentence's purpose first.

### 1. The basic rule

In traditional school grammar, wishes and prayers are commonly reported using phrases such as:
- **wished**
- **prayed**
- **blessed**
- **expressed the wish that**

The structure often depends on the meaning of the original sentence. <br/>
**Common pattern for wishes: `Subject + wished + object + to + verb`** <br/>
**Common pattern for prayers: `Subject + prayed + that + clause`**

These are useful patterns, but not every wish follows the same structure.

```
Direct speech:           The teacher said to me, "May you succeed in life!"
Indirect speech:         The teacher wished that I might succeed in life.
Another natural version: The teacher wished me success in life.

|               Direct speech	                          |          Indirect speech                         |
|-------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| He said to me, "May you be happy!"	                  | He wished that I might be happy.                 |
| She said to her son, "May you achieve your goals!"	  | She wished that her son might achieve his goals. |
| My friend said to me, "May you have a bright future!"	| My friend wished me a bright future.             |

Note: Traditional exercises often use `might` after `wished that`. <br/>
In everyday English, `hoped that` or `wished that … would` may be more natural in some contexts.

### 3. Prayers using `"May God…"`

When a sentence expresses a prayer, we commonly use `prayed that` or `prayed to God that`.

```
Direct:   The woman said, "May God bless my family!"
Indirect: The woman prayed that God might bless her family.

Direct:   He said, "May God protect us!"
Indirect: He prayed that God might protect them.

Direct:   The people said, "May God grant us peace!"
Indirect: The people prayed that God might grant them peace.
```

Notice the changes:
- `May` commonly becomes `might` in traditional school transformations.
- Pronouns change according to who is speaking and who is being referred to.
- The sentence becomes a statement clause after `that`.

### 4. Blessings

A blessing is a wish for good fortune, health, or protection. <br/>
The verb `blessed` can be suitable when someone is giving a blessing.

```
Direct:   The priest said to the child, "May God bless you!"
Indirect: The priest prayed that God might bless the child.
Indirect: The priest blessed the child.
- Alternatively, if the focus is the act of giving a blessing.
```
These versions are related but not identical: `prayed that God might bless the child` reports the prayer, while `blessed the child` describes the act of blessing.

### 5. Wishes using `"Long live…"`

Some wishes use the expression `"Long live…"`, meaning that someone or something should continue to live or flourish.

```
Direct:   The crowd said, "Long live our country!"
Indirect: The crowd wished that their country might live long.
Indirect: The crowd expressed the wish that their country would prosper.
- 2nd one is more natural alternative in modern English
```
In exams, follow the transformation pattern, while remembering that natural English can offer more than one correct rendering.

### 6. Quick comparison

| Purpose	      | Reporting phrase   | Example                             |
|---------------|--------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Personal wish	| `wished that`	     | She wished that I might succeed.    |
| Prayer	      | `prayed that`	     | He prayed that God might help them. |
| Blessing	    | `blessed`	         | The priest blessed the child.       |
| General hope	| `hoped that`	     | They hoped that we would succeed.   |

Important: Don't change every sentence containing `may` into a wish. 
For example, `"May I come in?"` is a request for permission, not a prayer. 
Its reported form is `"He asked if he might come in."`

-  The teacher wished that I might succeed in life.
-  This expresses a wish, so wished that is appropriate.

-  The women prayed that God might protect her children.
-  This is a prayer. "My" changes to "her" bcuz the woman is reporting her own words.

-  The priest prayed that God might bless the child.
-  Prayed that reports the blessing as a prayer. The "child" replaces "you" in this context.

-  The people prayed that peace might prevail

```

### 8. Quick recap

Identify the purpose before transforming the sentence:
- **Wish**: `wished that…`
- **Prayer**: `prayed that…`
- **Blessing**: `blessed someone`, or `prayed that God might bless them`.
- **Permission question**: `asked if/whether…`
Then check pronouns, verb forms, and the meaning of the complete sentence.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 21. Suggestions using <code> suggested </code> and <code> proposed </code> </b> </summary>

<br/>

We have learned how to report **wishes and prayers**. <br/>
Now, we'll learn how to report **suggestions - when someone recommends an idea or proposes a course of action**.

Examples:
```
"Let's go for a walk."
"Why don't we start early?"
"We should take a taxi."
"I suggest that we call the teacher."
```
The most important rule in this lesson is that **suggested is not normally followed by `a person + to-infinitive`**.
```
Incorrect: He suggested me to take a taxi.
Correct:   He suggested taking a taxi.
```
Let's learn the patterns step by step.


### 1. Pattern one: `suggested + verb-ing`

Use this pattern when reporting an activity or action being recommended.

**Structure: `Subject + suggested + verb-ing`**
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "Let's go to the park."
Indirect: Ravi suggested going to the park.
```
Examples:
| Direct speech	                      | Indirect speech                    |
|-------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| She said, "Let's watch a movie."    | She suggested watching a movie.    |
| He said, "Let's start the meeting."	| He suggested starting the meeting. |
| Priya said, "Let's have some tea."	| Priya suggested having some tea.   |

Here, Let's usually means `"Let us"` and proposes a shared activity. <br/>
In reported speech, we often omit it and use the `-ing` form.

### 2. Pattern two: `suggested + that-clause`

Use this structure when you want to state the suggestion as a complete clause.

**Structure: `Subject + suggested + that + subject + base verb`** <br/>
The `base verb` is common in formal English, especially in American English.

Examples:
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "We should revise this chapter."
Indirect: The teacher suggested that we revise that chapter.

Direct:   Riya said, "We should leave early."
Indirect: Riya suggested that we leave early.

Direct:   He said, "You should consult a doctor."
Indirect: He suggested that I consult a doctor. (Assuming I was the person he addressed.)
Indirect: He suggested that I should consult a doctor.
- In British English, it's also common to use should 2nd one.

Both patterns are grammatically correct.
```

### 3. Pattern three: `suggested + that-clause` with a changed viewpoint

Pronouns must change according to who made the suggestion and who was being addressed.
```
Direct:   Aman said to me, "You should apply for the job."
Indirect: Aman suggested that I should apply for the job.

Direct:   The manager said to the team, "You should finish the report today."
Indirect: The manager suggested that the team should finish the report that day.
```
Notice that we don't mechanically change `you` to `they` or `I`. We first identify the person or group the original speaker addressed.


### 4. Pattern four: `proposed`

Proposed is similar to suggested, but it often sounds more formal. <br/>
It is frequently used for plans, arrangements, or ideas put forward for consideration.

Common structures:
- **`proposed + verb-ing`**
- **`proposed that + clause`**

Examples:
| Direct speech	                                          | Indirect speech                                   |
|---------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| He said, "Let's build a library."	                      | He proposed building a library.                   |
| She said, "Let's postpone the meeting."	                | She proposed postponing the meeting.              |
| The chairman said, "We should introduce a new policy."	| The chairman proposed that a new policy should be introduced. |

The passive construction in the last example is natural because the focus is on the policy, not the person who would introduce it.

### 5. What about `"Why don't we…?"` and `"Shall we…?"`

These expressions often introduce suggestions.

Examples
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "Why don't we take a break?"
Indirect: Ravi suggested taking a break.

Direct:   Meena said, "Shall we begin the lesson?"
Indirect: Meena suggested beginning the lesson.

Direct:   The teacher said, "Why don't you practise every day?"
Indirect: The teacher suggested that I should practise every day. (Assuming the teacher was speaking to me.)
```
The reporting form depends on the intention: if the speaker is suggesting an action, use a suggestion pattern rather than reporting the original words as a literal question.

### 6. Common mistakes

| Incorrect	                            | Correct                           |
|---------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| ~~He suggested me to rest.~~	        | He suggested that I rest.         |
| ~~She suggested to go early.~~	      | She suggested going early.        |
| ~~He proposed me to join the team.~~	| He proposed that I join the team. |
| ~~She suggested that we went early.~~	| She suggested that we go early. (For a recommendation.) |

One useful distinction:
- **`Suggest + verb-ing`**: She suggested leaving early.
- **`Suggest + that-clause`**: She suggested that we leave early.
- **`Suggest + object + to-infinitive`**: Generally incorrect in standard English.
  
However, recommend has a similar pattern to suggest: “She recommended that I leave early” or “She recommended leaving early.”

### 7. Practice quiz

```
1. Ravi said, "Let's go for a walk."
-  Ravi suggested going for a walk
-  Suggested is naturally followed by a gerund (-ing form) when naming an activity.

2. The teacher said to me, "You should revise this chapter."
-  The teacher suggested that I should revise that chapter
-  Suggested + that-clause is correct. This may become that when the context calls for a demonstrative change.

3. The chairman said, "We should introduce a new rule."
-  The chairman proposed introducing a new rule.
-  Proposed + verb-ing is a correct way to report the proposed action.

4. Which sentence is grammatically correct?
a. She suggested me to wait.
b. She suggested to wait.
c. She suggested that I wait.
-  She suggested that I wait.
-  Suggested that I wait is correct; the base verb wait is used in this recommendation.

5. Meena said, "Shall we start now?"
-  Meena suggested starting then.
-  When Shall we...? expresses a suggestion, suggested + verb-ing is a natural report.
-  Now may become then depending on context.
```


### Quick recap

Remember these three rules:
1. **`Suggested + verb-ing`**: He suggested leaving early.
2. **`Suggested + that-clause`**: He suggested that we leave early.
3. **`Proposed + verb-ing / that-clause`**: She proposed changing the plan.

The most common error is using `suggested me to do something`.  use a `gerund` or a `that-clause` instead.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 22. Offers, promises, threats, and warnings </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll learn how to report **what someone offers to do, promises to do, threatens to do, or warns someone about**.

These sentences are slightly different from ordinary statements because the reporting verb tells us the speaker's intention.

###  1. Offers

An `offer` means someone volunteers or agrees to do something to help another person.

**Rule: `offered + to + base verb`**
```
Direct speech:   Ravi said to me, "I will help you with your homework."
Indirect speech: Ravi offered to help me with my homework.

Direct speech:   "I will carry your bag," she said.
Indirect speech: She offered to carry my bag.

Direct speech:   "Shall I open the window?" he said.
Indirect speech: He offered to open the window.

Direct speech:   "Can I give you a lift?" Ravi said.
Indirect speech: Ravi offered to give me a lift.
```
Tip: When someone volunteers to do something, `offered to + verb` is usually the most natural reporting pattern.

### 2. Promises

A `promise` means someone gives an assurance that they will do something or that something will happen.

There are two useful patterns:
| Pattern	                   | Example                            |
|----------------------------|------------------------------------|
| `promised + to-infinitive` | He promised to help me.            |
| `promised + that-clause`	 | He promised that he would help me. |

```
Direct speech:   Meena said, "I will return your book tomorrow."
Indirect speech: Meena promised to return my book the next day.

Direct speech:   "I will never lie to you," he said.
Indirect speech: He promised never to lie to me.

Direct speech:   "I will pay you back," she said.
Indirect speech: She promised that she would pay me back.

Direct speech:   "I won't be late," Aman said.
Indirect speech: Aman promised not to be late.
```
Notice that the original word `will` often becomes `would` in a `that-clause` after a past reporting verb. <br/>
With `promised to`, the word `would` is not needed.

### 3. Threats

A `threat` means someone says they will cause harm, punishment, or an unpleasant consequence if something happens or someone does not obey.

```
Direct speech:   The man said to the boy, "I will punish you if you break the window again."
Indirect speech: The man threatened to punish the boy if he broke the window again.
```

**Rule: `threatened + to + base verb`**

Examples:
```
Direct speech:   "I will tell your parents!" she said.
Indirect speech: She threatened to tell my parents.

Direct speech:   "I will report you to the principal," the teacher said.
Indirect speech: The teacher threatened to report me to the principal.

Direct speech:   "I will not spare you," he said.
Indirect speech: He threatened not to spare me.
```

A threat can also be reported using a `that-clause`:
```
Direct:   He said, "I will take revenge."
Indirect: He threatened that he would take revenge.
```

Important distinction, Compare these two sentences:
```
Direct:   "I will help you," she said.
Indirect: She offered to help me.

Direct:   "I will punish you," she said.
Indirect: She threatened to punish me.
```
Both use `will` in direct speech, but the intention is different. Choose the reporting verb according to the meaning, not merely the tense.


### 4. Warnings

A `warning` tells someone about danger or a possible problem, often to prevent harm.

```
Direct speech:   The teacher said to us, “Don't touch the electric wire.”
Indirect speech: The teacher warned us not to touch the electric wire.
```

Two common patterns are:
| Pattern	                             | Example                                   |
|--------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| `warned + object + to/not to + verb` | She warned me not to go outside.          |
| `warned + object + that + clause`    | She warned me that the road was slippery. |

Examples:
```
Direct speech:   "Be careful! The floor is wet," he said to me.
Indirect speech: He warned me to be careful, explaining that the floor was wet.

Direct speech:   "You may lose your money," she said to him.
Indirect speech: She warned him that he might lose his money.

Direct speech:   "Don't go near the dog," my father said to me.
Indirect speech: My father warned me not to go near the dog.
```

**Warning vs. threat**
- **Warning**: The speaker alerts someone to danger or a possible consequence.
- **Threat**: The speaker indicates an intention to cause harm or impose a consequence.

Example:
```
Direct speech:   "Don't touch that wire; you may get an electric shock."
Indirect speech:  He warned me not to touch the wire.

Direct speech:   "Touch that wire again, and I will punish you."
Indirect speech:  He threatened to punish me.
```
The exact reporting verb depends on the context. Some sentences can be interpreted in more than one way.


### 5. Quick Revision

| Meaning	                            | Reporting verb   |    Main pattern                     |
|-------------------------------------|------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Offering help	                      | `offered`	       | `offered + to + verb`               |
| Giving assurance	                  | `promised`       | `promised + to + verb`              |
| Threatening harm	                  | `threatened`     | `threatened + to + verb`            |
| Alerting someone to danger	        | `warned`	       | `warned + object + to/not to + verb`|
| Warning about a fact or consequence	| `warned`	       | `warned + that + clause`            |

Remember: pronouns, tense, and time expressions may also change where necessary. <br/>
For example, `tomorrow` may become `the next day` when the reporting time is later.

**Takeaway**

When we learn these expressions, first ask: `"What is the speaker trying to do?"`
- Offering help -> **`offered to`**
- Giving an assurance -> **`promised to`**
- Threatening a consequence -> **`threatened to`**
- Alerting someone to danger -> **`warned someone to/not to`**, or **`warned someone that…`**

### 6. Practice quiz

```
1. Riya said to me, "I will carry your bag."
-  Riya offered to carry my bag.
-  She volunteers to help, so offered to carry is the best choice.

2. Mohan said, "I will return your money tomorrow."
-  Mohan promised to return my money the next day.
-  He gives an assurance, so promised to return is correct.

-  The man threatened to call the police if I did that again.
-  In this context, the statement is a threat. Threatened to call is the appropriate pattern.

  <summary> <b> 23. Greetings, thanks, apologies, congratulations, and goodbyes </b> </summary>

<br/>

We'll learn how to report common expressions like **Greetings, Thanks, Apologies, Congratulations, and Goodbyes** used in everyday conversation. 

These sentences often do not follow the usual statement or question patterns. Instead, we choose a reporting verb that communicates the speaker's intention.

Example:
```
Direct:  "Thank you for helping me!"
Indirect: She thanked me for helping her.

Direct:  "I'm sorry for my mistake."
Indirect: He apologised for his mistake.

Direct:  "Congratulations on your success!"
Indirect: She congratulated me on my success.
```

Let's learn each category step by step.

### 1. Greetings

`Greetings` include expressions such as **Hello, Good morning, and Good evening**.

```
Direct Speech:   The teacher said to the students, "Good morning, everyone!"
Indirect Speech: The teacher greeted the students.
```
Examples:
```
He said to me, "Hello!"
He greeted me.

She said to her neighbour, "Good evening!"
She greeted her neighbour.
```
**Rule: `greeted + person`**

We generally don't need to preserve the exact greeting word when reporting it indirectly. We report the action of greeting.


### 2. Thanks and expressions of gratitude

When someone says `"Thank you,"` we commonly use the reporting verb thanked.

```
Direct Speech:   Rohan said to me, "Thank you for lending me your book."
Indirect Speech: Rohan thanked me for lending him my book.
```

**Rule: `thanked + person + for + noun / verb-ing`**

Examples:
```
"Thank you for your help," she said to Ravi.
She thanked Ravi for his help.

"Thanks for waiting," he said to us.
He thanked us for waiting.

"Thank you for inviting me," Priya said.
Priya thanked the speaker for inviting her.
```

Common mistake
```
Incorrect: She thanked me to help her.
Correct:   She thanked me for helping her.
```
Use `for + noun` or `for + verb-ing`, `not to + verb`, to describe the reason for thanking someone.

### 3. Apologies

An `apology` expresses regret for something wrong or inconvenient. <br/>
Common expressions include `"I'm sorry"`, `"I apologise,"` and `"Please forgive me."`

The reporting verb is usually `apologised` (British/Indian English) or `apologized` (American English).
```
Direct Speech:   Amit said to me, "I am sorry for breaking your pen."
Indirect Speech: Amit apologised to me for breaking my pen.
```

Patterns to remember:
- **`apologised to + person`**
- **`apologised to + person + for + noun / verb-ing`**

Examples:
```
"I'm sorry for being late," she said to the teacher.
She apologised to the teacher for being late.

"I apologise for my mistake," he said.
He apologised for his mistake.

"Please forgive me," Ravi said to his friend.
Ravi asked his friend to forgive him.
```
Important: We usually say `apologised to me`, not `apologised me`. The preposition to is required before the person receiving the apology. 


### 4. Congratulations

We `congratulate`someone when they achieve something or experience a happy event.

```
Direct Speech:   Neha said to Rahul, "Congratulations on winning the competition!"
Indirect Speech: Neha congratulated Rahul on winning the competition.
```

**Rule: `congratulated + person + on + noun / verb-ing`**

Examples:
```
Direct:  "Congratulations on your promotion!" she said to him.
Indirect: She congratulated him on his promotion.

Direct:  "Well done! You have passed the exam," the teacher said to me.
Indirect: The teacher congratulated me on passing the exam.

Direct:  "Congratulations on getting the job!" he said to Priya.
Indirect: He congratulated Priya on getting the job.
```

Common mistake
```
Incorrect: She congratulated me for winning.
Correct:   She congratulated me on winning.  [Preferred standard pattern]
```
Remember the combination congratulate someone on something.

### 5. Goodbyes and farewells

When someone says `"Goodbye"`, `"Bye"`, or `"See you soon"`, we can use **bade farewell**, **said goodbye to**, or another expression that conveys the intended meaning.

```
Direct Speech:   The students said to their teacher, "Goodbye, sir!"
Indirect Speech: The students said goodbye to their teacher.
```

Examples:
```
Direct:   She said to me, "Goodbye!"
Indirect: She said goodbye to me.

Direct:   The guests said, "Farewell!"
Indirect: The guests bade farewell. (A formal expression.)

Direct:   Ravi said to his friend, "See you tomorrow!"
Indirect: Ravi said he would see his friend the next day.
```
The last example reports the meaning of the expression rather than using a fixed reporting verb.


### 6. Practice quiz

```
1. Meena said to me, "Thank you for helping me."
-  Meena thanked me for helping her.
-  Use thanked + person + for + verb-ing.
- The pronoun "me" changes to "her" bcuz Meena is reporting her own action.

2. Ravi said to the teacher, "I'm sorry for coming late."
-  Ravi apologised to the teacher for coming late.
-  An apology uses apologised to + person + for + verb-ing.

3. The coach said to the team, "Congratulations on winning the match!"
-  The coach congratulated the team on winning the match.
-  The standard pattern is congratulated + person + on + noun or verb-ing.

4. Sita said to her friend, "Goodbye!"
-  Sita said goodbye to her friend.
-  Goodbye is reported naturally using said goodbye to + person.

5. The principal said to the students, "Good morning!"
-  The principal greeted the students
-  A greeting can be reported using greeted + person.
```

### 7. Quick Recap

| Expression	               | Reporting pattern                             |
|----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **Hello! / Good morning!** | `greeted + person`                            |
| **Thank you!**	           | `thanked + person + for + noun/verb-ing`      |
| **I'm sorry!**	           | `apologised to + person + for + noun/verb-ing`|
| **Please forgive me!**	   | `asked + person + to forgive`                 |
| **Congratulations!**	     | `congratulated + person + on + noun/verb-ing` |
| **Goodbye!**	             | `said goodbye to + person`

These patterns preserve the speaker's meaning. Pronouns and possessives may change according to who is speaking, who is listening, and who is reporting.

**Takeaway**

The easiest method is to identify the purpose of the expression first:
- **Greeting** -> `greet`
- **Giving thanks** -> `thank`
- **Expressing regret** -> `apologise`
- **Celebrating someone's achievement** -> `congratulate`
- **Saying goodbye** -> `say goodbye`

Then take the prepositions carefully: `thank someone for`, `apologise to someone for`, and `congratulate someone on`.

<details>
  <summary> <b> 24. Modal verbs: <code> can, may, must, should, would </code>, and others </b> </summary>

<br/>

**Modal Verbs in Direct and Indirect Speech**

> Modal verbs are helping verbs that express ability, permission, possibility, obligation, advice, or willingness.

They include `can, could, may, might, must, should, will, would, and ought to`.

When changing direct speech into indirect speech, some modals change form, while others usually remain unchanged. <br/> 
The key is to understand the original meaning and the reporting context.

### 1. The main rules for modal verbs

It shows the usual transformations taught in school grammar when the reporting verb is in the past.

| Direct speech	| Indirect speech	 | Example                                  |
|---------------|------------------|------------------------------------------|
| `can`	        | `could`	         | "I can swim." -> He said he could swim.  |
| `may`	        | `might`	         | "I may come." -> She said she might come.|
| `will`	      | `would`	         | "I will help." -> He said he would help. |
| `shall`	      | `should / would` | Depends on the meaning and subject       |
| `must`	      | `had to / must`	 | Depends on the meaning                   |
| `could`	      | `could`	         | Usually unchanged                        |
| `might`	      | `might`	         | Usually unchanged                        |
| `should`	    | `should`	       | Usually unchanged                        |
| `would`       |	`would`	         | Usually unchanged                        |
| `ought to`	  | `ought to`	     | Usually unchanged                        |

Important: These are common patterns, not mechanical rules for every situation. <br/>
If the reporting verb is present or the statement remains true, a modal may not need to change.

### 2. `Can` -> `Could`

We commonly change `can` to `could` when reporting a statement after a past-tense reporting verb.

```
Direct Speech:   Ravi said, "I can solve this problem."
Indirect Speech: Ravi said that he could solve that problem.

Direct:   She said, "I can drive a car."
Indirect: She said that she could drive a car.

Direct:   Amit said, "I cannot attend the meeting."
Indirect: Amit said that he could not attend the meeting.
- Notice that "cannot" becomes "could not" in the usual backshift pattern.
```

**When `can` remains `can`** <br/>
If the reporting verb is in the present, we normally retain the modal:
```
- Direct:   Ravi says, "I can swim."
- Indirect: Ravi says that he can swim.
```

### 3. `May` -> `Might`

`May` often expresses possibility or permission. <br/>
After a past reporting verb, `may` commonly changes to `might`.

```
Direct:   Neha said, "I may visit Delhi tomorrow."
Indirect: Neha said that she might visit Delhi the next day.

Direct:   He said, "It may rain today."
Indirect: He said that it might rain that day.
```

However, `might` can already appear in direct speech and usually remains `might`:
```
Direct:   She said, "I might be late."
Indirect: She said that she might be late.
```

### 4. `Will` → `Would`

`Will` commonly changes to `would` after a past reporting verb.

```
Direct:   Priya said, "I will call you."
Indirect: Priya said that she would call me.

Negative form:
Direct:   Ravi said, "I will not forget."
Indirect: Ravi said that he would not forget.
```
Remember to change pronouns according to the speaker and listener. <br/>
In the first example, `you` becomes `me` only if the reporter is the person Priya was addressing.

### 5. `Shall` -> `Should` or `Would`

The transformation of `shall` depends on its meaning and the sentence structure.

**A. `Shall` expressing a future action**
```
Direct:   He said, "I shall return tomorrow."
Indirect: He said that he would return the next day.
```
**B. `Shall I…?` expressing an offer**
```
Direct:   She said to me, "Shall I open the window?"
Indirect: She offered to open the window.
```
**C. `Shall we…?` expressing a suggestion**
```
Direct:   He said, "Shall we start the meeting?"
Indirect: He suggested starting the meeting.
```

Tip: Don't change every instance of `shall` to `should`. First identify whether it expresses a future action, offer, suggestion, or another meaning.

### 6. `Must` -> `Had to` or `Must`

This is one of the most important rules because must can express different meanings.

**A. `Must` expressing obligation** <br/>
When `must` means that someone is required to do something, it commonly changes to `had to` in traditional school transformations.
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "You must finish your homework today."
Indirect: The teacher said that we had to finish our homework that day.

Direct: The officer said, "You must wear a helmet."
Indirect: The officer said that I had to wear a helmet.
- The pronoun depends on whom the officer addressed.
```

**B. `Must` expressing a continuing obligation** <br/>
`Must` can remain unchanged when the obligation still applies or the speaker wishes to preserve its force.
```
Direct:   The doctor said, "You must take this medicine regularly."
Indirect: The doctor said that I must take the medicine regularly.
```
In many school exercises, `had to` may be expected when the original reporting verb is in the past. <br/>
In real English, context determines which form sounds most appropriate.

**C. `Must` expressing a logical conclusion** <br/>
When `must` means `"I am almost certain,"` it often remains `must`.
```
Direct:   He said, "She must be tired."
Indirect: He said that she must be tired.
- Here, must expresses a deduction, not an obligation.
```

### 7. `Could`, `Might`, `Should`, `Would`, and `Ought to`

These modal verbs generally remain unchanged when reported.

| Direct speech	            | Indirect speech               |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| "I could swim."	          | He said he could swim.        |
| "It might rain."	        | She said it might rain.       |
| "You should study."	      | He said I should study.       |
| "I would help you."       | She said she would help me.   |
| "You ought to apologise."	| He said I ought to apologise. |

They may also appear differently when the context or meaning changes, but for standard school transformations, retaining them is the usual rule.


### 8. The important difference between `mustn't` and `don't have to`

These expressions do not mean the same thing.

- **`mustn't` = prohibited; you are not allowed to do it**.
- **`don't have to` = it is not necessary to do it**.
Examples:
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "You mustn't copy during the exam."
Indirect: The teacher said that we mustn't copy during the exam.
- The prohibition remains a prohibition.

Direct:   She said, "You don't have to wait for me."
Indirect: She said that I didn't have to wait for her.
- Don't change mustn't to didn't have to, bcuz that reverses the meaning.
```

### 9. Practice quiz

```
1. Ravi said, "I can solve this problem."
-  Ravi said that he could solve that problem.
-  Can commonly changes to could after a past reporting verb.
-  The main verb remains in its base form.

2. Neha said, "I may visit you tomorrow."
-  Neha said that she might visit me the next day.
-  May commonly changes to might, tomorrow becomes the next day,
-  and you becomes me if Neha was speaking to the reporter.

3. The teacher said, "You must submit your work today."
-  The teacher said that we had to submit our work that day.
-  Must expresses obligation here, so had to is the usual school-grammar transformation.

 4. He said, "She might arrive late."
-   He said that she might arrive late.
-   Might usually remains unchanged in indirect speech.

5. The guard said, "You mustn't enter this room."
-  The guard said that we mustn't enter that room.
-  Mustn't expresses prohibition,
-  so it should not be changed to didn't have to, which means there is no obligation.
```

### 10. Quick Recap

| Modal Verbs          | description                      |
|----------------------|----------------------------------|
| `can` -> `could`     | Ability                          |
| `may` -> `might`     | Possibility or permission        |
| `will` -> `would`    |  Future action or willingness    |
| `must` -> `had to`   | Usually, past-reported obligation|
| `should` -> `should` | Usually unchanged                |


Before changing a modal verb, ask three questions:
**1. Is the reporting verb in the past?** <br/>
**2. Does the modal express ability, possibility, future action, obligation, or prohibition?** <br/>
**3. Does the original meaning still apply, or is the exercise testing a traditional backshift rule?** <br/>

These questions prevent us from treating modal verbs as a list of automatic replacements.

Universal truths, habitual facts, and scientific facts

<details>
  <summary> <b> 25. Universal truths, habitual facts, and scientific facts </b> </summary>

<br/>

We have learned that when the reporting verb is in the past, **the tense of the reported speech often shifts backwards**.

Example:
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "I am tired."
Indirect: Ravi said that he was tired.
```

But does every sentence have to change its tense? <br/>
**No. Universal truths, scientific facts, and statements that remain true often stay in the present tense**, even when the reporting verb is in the past.

Let's understand the rules and the exceptions.

### 1. Universal truths

A universal truth is a fact that is generally accepted as true regardless of who reports it or when it is reported.


Example 1: The Earth rotates
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "The Earth rotates on its axis."
Indirect: The teacher said that the Earth rotates on its axis.
```
Example 2: Plants need sunlight
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "Plants need sunlight to grow."
Indirect: The teacher said that plants need sunlight to grow.
- Notice that rotates and need remain in the present tense.
```

**Rule**
When the reported statement expresses a universal truth, the present tense is normally retained.
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "The Sun rises in the east."
Indirect: The teacher said that the Sun rises in the east.

Direct:   He said, "Water freezes at 0°C under standard atmospheric pressure."
Indirect: He said that water freezes at 0°C under standard atmospheric pressure.
```
Tip: Ask, `"Is this fact generally true, or was it true only at that particular time?"` This helps them decide whether the tense should change. 


### 2. Scientific facts

Scientific facts describe established scientific principles, laws, and processes.

Examples:
```
Direct speech:   The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
Indirect speech: The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.

Direct speech:   The scientist said, "Light travels faster than sound."
Indirect speech: The scientist said that light travels faster than sound.

Direct speech:   The teacher said, "Water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure."
Indirect speech: The teacher said that water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.
```
The present tense remains because the statements describe facts that continue to hold true under the stated conditions.


### 3. Habitual facts and repeated actions

A habitual fact describes something that happens regularly. However, not every habit must remain in the present tense. We need to consider whether the habit is still true when we report it.

**Case A: A habit that is still true**
```
Direct: Rohan said, "I walk to school every day."
Indirect: Rohan said that he walks to school every day. [If Rohan still walks to school every day:]
- The present tense is appropriate because the habit continues.
```

**Case B: A habit viewed from the past**
```
Direct: Rohan said, "I walk to school every day."
Indirect: Rohan said that he walked to school every day.
- If Rohan was describing his old routine, or we are simply reporting his words from a past viewpoint:
```
Both forms can be correct, depending on the intended meaning.

```
Direct:   Meera said, "My father reads the newspaper every morning."
Indirect: Meera said that her father reads the newspaper every morning. [If the habit continues]
Indirect: Meera said that her father read the newspaper every morning.  [If reporting the habit as a past routine]
```

### 4. Statements that are still true

A statement does not have to be a scientific fact to remain in the present tense. It may simply describe a situation that is still true.

Compare these examples:

**Still true**
```
Direct:   Priya said, "I live in Delhi."
Indirect: Priya said that she lives in Delhi.
- Use the present tense if she still lives there and you want to communicate that current fact.
```

**Past situation**
```
Direct:   Priya said, "I live in Delhi."
Indirect: Priya said that she lived in Delhi.
- The past tense is suitable if you are reporting her situation from a past viewpoint or
- do not intend to assert that she still lives there.
```

This is an important distinction between school grammar and everyday English. <br/> 
Traditional transformation exercises often expect backshift after `said`, but natural English allows the present tense when the information remains current.

### 5. When should the tense change?

Use this comparison to help us make the right decision.

| Type of statement	                      | Usual approach                             |
|-----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Universal truth	                        | Usually retain the present tense           |
| Established scientific fact	            | Usually retain the present tense           |
| Habit that continues today	            | Present tense is possible                  |
| Past habit that has ended	              | Past tense is appropriate                  |
| Personal feeling or temporary situation	| Usually backshift in traditional exercises |
| Statement with a past reporting verb in a standard school exercise | Usually backshift unless there is a reason not to |

Remember: **meaning and context matter more than memorising a single rule**.


### 6. Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Changing every tense automatically**
```
Incorrect for the intended universal-truth meaning:
- The teacher said that the Earth revolved around the Sun.
Preferred: The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
- The past form may be grammatically possible in a different context,
- but it is not the usual choice for reporting a continuing scientific fact.
```

**Mistake 2: Assuming every repeated action is a universal truth**
```
Direct: Aman said, "I play cricket every Sunday."
- This is a personal habit, not a universal truth.
- Depending on the context, both of these are possible:
Indirect: Aman said that he played cricket every Sunday. [If the habit was in past.]
Indirect: Aman said that he plays cricket every Sunday.  [If the habit continues.]
```

**Mistake 3: Ignoring the conditions of a scientific fact**
```
Direct:    The teacher said, "Water boils at 100°C."
Preferred: The teacher said that water boils at 100°C under standard atmospheric pressure.
- Scientific statements sometimes depend on conditions, so preserve those conditions when they matter.
```

### 7. Practice quiz

Consider whether the sentence describes a universal fact, a continuing habit, or a past situation.

```
1. The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
-  The teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun.
-  The sentence expresses a continuing scientific fact, so the present tense is normally retained.

2. Neha said, "I go for a walk every morning." [Her routine has not changed.]
-  Neha said that she goes for a walk every morning.
-  Since the habit continues, the present tense is appropriate.

3. Ravi said, "I am tired."
-  Ravi said that he was tired.
-  For a typical school transformation, the present continuous form of be (am) backshifts
-  to was when the reporting verb is past.

4. The scientist said, "Light travels faster than sound."
-  The scientist said that light travels faster than sound.
-  This is an established scientific fact, so the present tense is normally retained.

5. Sita said, "I play tennis every weekend." [She stopped playing last year.]
-  Sita said that she played tennis every weekend.
-  The habit has ended, so the past tense appropriately describes her former routine.
```

### 8. Quick Recap summary

The rules to remember is:
- **Universal truth**: usually keep the present tense.
- **Scientific fact**: usually keep the present tense.
- **Continuing habit or current fact**: present tense may be retained.
- **Past or temporary situation**: backshift is often appropriate after a past reporting verb.

For examinations, we should follow the specific rules taught in their syllabus. <br/>
For real communication, they should also consider whether the statement remains true.

<br/>

So far, we've learned reporting verbs such as `said, told, asked, advised, promised, and suggested`. <br/>
Now we'll learn a more advanced skill: **choosing the correct grammatical structure after each reporting verb**.

This is important because two verbs can have similar meanings but require different sentence patterns. <br/>
Example: <br/>
He **admitted stealing** the money. <br/>
He **denied stealing** the money. <br/>
He **accused me of stealing** the money. <br/>
He **warned me not to steal** the money.


### 1. The five main sentence patterns

| Pattern 1                           |
|-------------------------------------|
| **Verb + to-infinitive**            |
| `verb + to + base verb`             |
| Example: `She promised to help me.` |


| Pattern 2                               |
|-----------------------------------------|
| **Verb + verb-ing**                     |
| `verb + verb-ing`                       |
| Example: `He admitted taking the money.`|


| Pattern 3                               |
|-----------------------------------------|
| **Verb + object + to-infinitive**       |
| `verb + person + to + base verb`        |
| Example: `She advised me to study.`     |


| Pattern 4                                   |
|---------------------------------------------|
| **Verb + preposition + verb-ing**           |
| `verb + preposition + verb-ing`             |
| Example: `He apologised for arriving late.` |


| Pattern 5                                  |
|--------------------------------------------|
| **Verb + that-clause**                     |
| `verb + that + subject + verb`             |
| Example: `She explained that she was busy.`|

These are common patterns, not an exhaustive list. Some reporting verbs can use more than one pattern depending on their meaning.

### 2. Reporting verbs followed by verb-ing

Some verbs are commonly followed by a **gerund** (the `-ing` form of a verb).

**A. Admit** <br/>
Admit means to acknowledge that something is true, often something wrong.
```
Direct:   He said, "I stole the money."
Indirect: He admitted stealing the money.
Indirect: He admitted that he had stolen the money. [Also correct]
```
**Pattern: `admitted + verb-ing or admitted + that-clause`**

**B. Deny** <br/>
Deny means to say that something is not true or that you did not do something.
```
Direct:   She said, "I didn't break the vase."
Indirect: She denied breaking the vase.
Indirect: She denied that she had broken the vase. [Also correct]
```
**Pattern: `denied + verb-ing or denied + that-clause`**

**C. Suggest** <br/>
Suggest means to put forward an idea or possible action.
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "Let's take a break."
Indirect: Ravi suggested taking a break.
Indirect: Ravi suggested that we take a break.   [Also correct]
```
**Pattern: `suggested + verb-ing or suggested that + clause`**

**Common mistake**:
Do not write suggested me to take a break in standard English. <br/>
Use `suggested taking a break` or `suggested that I take a break.`

### 3. Reporting verbs followed by `object + to-infinitive`

These verbs are often used when someone tells, encourages, invites, or persuades another person to do something.

| Reporting verb | Meaning	                  | Example                           |
|----------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| `advised`	     | recommended an action	    | She advised me to rest.           |
| `encouraged`   | gave confidence or support	| He encouraged me to try.          |
| `persuaded`	   | convinced someone	        | They persuaded him to stay.       |
| `reminded`	   | helped someone remember	  | She reminded me to call.          |
| `invited`	     | asked someone to join	    | He invited us to attend.          |
| `warned`	     | alerted someone to danger	| She warned me not to touch it.    |
| `ordered`	     | gave a command	            | The officer ordered them to stop. |

Examples in indirect speech
```
Direct:   The doctor said to me, "Take some rest."
Indirect: The doctor advised me to take some rest.

Direct:   My friend said to me, "Please come to my birthday party."
Indirect: My friend invited me to come to her birthday party.

Direct:   The teacher said to Ravi, "Don't waste your time."
Indirect: The teacher warned Ravi not to waste his time.
```
Remember: These verbs generally require the person being addressed before the infinitive: `advised me to`, `persuaded him to`, `reminded her to`.


### 4. Reporting verbs followed by a preposition

Some reporting verbs require a specific preposition before the person or action. <br/>
Learn the entire combination rather than memorising the verb alone.

| Reporting verb pattern	                | Example                              |
|-----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| `accused + person + of + verb-ing`	    | She accused him of lying.            |
| `apologised + for + verb-ing`	          | He apologised for arriving late.     |
| `congratulated + person + on + verb-ing`| I congratulated her on winning.      |
| `thanked + person + for + verb-ing`	    | She thanked me for helping.          |
| `insisted + on + verb-ing`	            | He insisted on paying.               |
| `blamed + person + for + noun/verb-ing`	| She blamed him for breaking the vase.|

Worked examples

**Example 1: Accused**
```
Direct: Riya said to Mohan, "You stole my pen!"
Indirect: Riya accused Mohan of stealing her pen.
```
Why? The pattern is `accused + person + of + verb-ing`.

**Example 2: Apologised**
```
Direct: Aman said, “I am sorry for disturbing you.”
Indirect: Aman apologised for disturbing me.
```
Why? The pattern is `apologised + for + verb-ing`.

**Example 3: Insisted**
```
Direct: Rahul said, “I will pay the bill myself.”
Indirect: Rahul insisted on paying the bill himself.
```
Why? In this meaning, `insisted` on is followed by `a noun` or `verb-ing` form.

A useful comparison:
```
He insisted on paying.  - He strongly maintained that he would pay.
He insisted that I pay. - He strongly demanded that I pay.
- Both are possible, but the subject and meaning of the action differ.
```


### 5. Reporting verbs followed by a that-clause

A `that-clause` contains a subject and a verb. It is useful when reporting a complete statement, belief, explanation, or claim.

Common examples include:

| Verb	       | Example                                    |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------|
| `explained`	 | She explained that she was busy.           |
| `claimed`	   | He claimed that he was innocent.           | 
| `admitted`	 | He admitted that he had made a mistake.    |
| `denied`	   | She denied that she had taken the money.   |
| `agreed`	   | They agreed that the plan was suitable.    |
| `complained` | He complained that the room was too noisy. |
| `promised`	 | She promised that she would return.        |
| `insisted`	 | He insisted that he was right.             |

Notice that `admit`, `deny`, and `insist` can take more than one structure. The structure often depends on the intended meaning.

Worked example
```
Direct:   The student said, "I didn't understand the question."
Indirect: The student explained that he hadn't understood the question.
- Here, explained introduces the reported statement, and
- the tense shifts in the usual way after the past reporting verb.
```

### 6. The most confusing verbs: `Say`, `tell`, `speak`, and `talk`

These verbs are not interchangeable in every structure.

| Correct pattern	               |   Example                          |
|--------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| `say + something`              | He said that he was tired.         |
| `say + something + to someone` | He said to me that he was tired.   |
| `tell + person + something`    | He told me that he was tired.      |
| `speak to + person`	           | He spoke to me about the problem.  |
| `talk to/with + person`	       | He talked to me about the problem. |

**Common errors**:
```
Incorrect: He said me that he was tired.
Correct:   He told me that he was tired.
Correct:   He said to me that he was tired.
```
Also remember that `told` normally needs an object in this reporting pattern: `told me`, `told her`, `told the students`.

- Accuse someone of something.

He apologised me for being late.  [Incorrect]
- He apologised to me for being late.
- Apologise to someone for something.

She persuaded me studying.        [Incorrect]
- She persuaded me to study.
- Persuade someone to do something.

He denied to take the money.       [Incorrect]
- He denied taking the money.
- Deny + verb-ing or deny + that-clause.
```

<details>
  <summary> <b> 27. Mixed tenses, conditional sentences, and complex reporting </b> </summary>

<br/>

We have learned to change one sentence at a time. <br/>
Now we'll practise sentences that contain more than one tense, conditional clauses, and several connected ideas.

The main principle is this: **change each part according to its meaning and grammatical role, rather than changing every verb mechanically**.

### 1. Mixed tenses in one sentence

A sentence may contain two or more clauses, each with its own tense.

Example:
```
Direct speech:   Ravi said, "I am studying because I have an exam tomorrow."
Indirect speech: Ravi said that he was studying because he had an exam the next day.

Let's break it down:
- "I am studying"           - present continuous.
- "I have an exam tomorrow" - present simple, with a future time reference.
```
What changed?
| Direct speech	| Indirect speech	| Reason                         |
|---------------|-----------------|--------------------------------|
| `I`	          | `he`	          | Pronoun refers to Ravi         |
| `am studying` | `was studying`	| Present continuous backshifts  |
| `have`	      | `had`	          | Present simple backshifts here |
| `tomorrow`	  | `the next day`	| Reporting time is later        |

Notice that each clause has been considered separately.

```
Direct:   Priya said, "I finished my homework before my mother came home."
Indirect: Priya said that she had finished her homework before her mother came home.
```
The earlier action, `had finished`, is expressed using the past perfect. The second past-tense verb, `came`, can remain in the simple past because the sequence is already clear.

### 2. Sentences with `because`, `although`, and `when`

Conjunctions connect clauses, but they do not automatically determine whether a tense changes.

**Because**
```
Direct:   Aman said, "I am staying home because I feel ill."
Indirect: Aman said that he was staying home because he felt ill.
```

**Although**
```
Direct:   Meena said, "Although I am tired, I will finish the work."
Indirect: Meena said that although she was tired, she would finish the work.
```

**When**
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "I was cooking when the lights went out."
Indirect: Ravi said that he had been cooking when the lights went out.
```

The last example uses a common school-style backshift for the ongoing earlier action, but retaining `was cooking` can also be natural in context. The goal is to preserve the relationship between the events.



### 3. Conditional sentences in reported speech

Conditional sentences express a condition and its possible result. They often contain `if` or `unless`.

There are three important types to understand.

**A. First conditional: a real future possibility**
```
Direct:   Rahul said, "If it rains, I will stay at home."
Indirect: Rahul said that if it rained, he would stay at home.
```
In a traditional school transformation, `rains` often changes to `rained` and `will` changes to `would`. <br/>
However, if the condition is still a real future possibility, a speaker may retain the present and future forms:
`Rahul said that if it rains, he will stay at home.`
The intended time and meaning determine which version is appropriate.

**B. Second conditional: an imagined or unlikely situation**
```
Direct:   Sita said, "If I had more money, I would buy a car."
Indirect: Sita said that if she had more money, she would buy a car.
```
The conditional structure generally remains unchanged because `had` is already the past form used for an unreal present situation, and `would` usually remains `would`.

**C. Third conditional: an unreal past situation**
```
Direct:   Ravi said, "If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam."
Indirect: Ravi said that if he had studied harder, he would have passed the exam.
```
The past perfect and `would have + past participle` generally remain unchanged.



### 4. `Unless`, `whether`, and other conditional expressions

Not every condition uses `if`.
- **`Unless`** means approximately **`"if not"`.**
- **`Whether`** can introduce alternatives or uncertainty.
- **`As long as`** can express a condition that must be satisfied.

Examples:

**Unless**
```
Direct:   The teacher said, "Unless you work hard, you will not pass."
Indirect: The teacher said that unless we worked hard, we would not pass.
```

**Whether**
```
Direct:   She said, "I don't know whether he will come."
Indirect: She said that she didn't know whether he would come.
```

**As long as**
```
Direct:   Father said, "You can go out as long as you finish your homework."
Indirect: Father said that I could go out as long as I finished my homework.
```

In each case, preserve the original relationship between the condition and the result.


### 5. Complex reporting: one sentence inside another

Sometimes the reported sentence already contains a reported statement or a clause introduced by `that`.

Example:
```
Direct:   Neha said, "I think that Ravi is honest."
Indirect: Neha said that she thought that Ravi was honest.
```

There are two clauses to consider:
1. `I think` becomes `she thought`.
2. `Ravi is honest` becomes `Ravi was honest` in the usual school-style backshift.

But `if Ravi is still known to be honest` and the reporter wishes to state this as a current fact, R`avi is honest may remain`.

```
Direct:   Mohan said, "I know that the train leaves at six."
Indirect: Mohan said that he knew that the train left at six.
- If the train's current timetable is being communicated, the reporter may retain leaves at six.
```


### 6. Reported speech with two reporting verbs

Sometimes a sentence includes a statement and a request, warning, or promise.

```
Direct:   The teacher said to Ravi, "You are late, so please sit down quietly."
Indirect: The teacher told Ravi that he was late and asked him to sit down quietly.
```
Here, we use two different reporting patterns:
- **Statement: `told + person + that-clause`**
- **Request: `asked + person + to-infinitive`**

```
Direct:   Mother said to me, "Don't go outside because it is raining."
Indirect: Mother told me not to go outside because it was raining.
```
The negative command becomes `not to go`, while the reason clause follows the normal tense rules.

### 7. A step-by-step method for complex sentences

Use this method when a sentence looks difficult.

| Step-by-step method                                                 |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **1. Identify the sentence type** <br/> Is it a statement, question, command, request, or mixed construction? |
| **2. Choose the reporting verb**  <br/> Use `said, asked, advised, warned, promised`, or another verb that fits the meaning. |
| **3. Change pronouns and references** <br/> Check who is speaking, who is being addressed, and whether time or place expressions need updating. |
| **4. Check each clause separately** <br/> Consider tense backshift, conditions, conjunctions, and any universal truths. |
| **5. Read for meaning** <br/> Make sure the final sentence preserves the original meaning and is grammatically correct. |


### 8. Practice quiz

These questions combine tenses, conditions, and reporting patterns.
```
1. Ravi said, "If it rains, I will stay at home."
-  Ravi said that if it rained, he would stay at home.
-  This is the usual school-style backshift for a first conditional:
- "rains" -> "rained" and "will stay" -> "would stay".

2. Priya said, "I was reading when my brother arrived."
-  Priya said that she had been reading when her brother arrived.
-  "was reading" commonly backshifts to "had been reading" in traditional school transformations.
-  Arrived can remain simple past to preserve the sequence.

3. The teacher said to Aman, "You are late, so sit down quietly."
-  The teacher told Aman that he was late and asked him to sit down quietly
-  The statement uses told + person + that-clause,
-  while the instruction uses asked + person + to-infinitive.

4. Sita said, "If I had studied harder, I would have passed."
-  Sita said that if she had studied harder, she would have passed.
-  The third conditional generally remains unchanged in reported speech.


5. Mohan said, "I know that the Earth revolves around the Sun."
-  Mohan said that he knew that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
-  The main clause backshifts to "knew",
-  while the universal scientific fact normally remains in the present tense.
```

### 9. Quick Recap and takeaway

When we encounter a long sentence, We have to divide it into clauses first. <br/>
Then apply the relevant rule to each clause.

Example:
```
"Although I am tired, I will finish the work because I have a deadline tomorrow."
Break it into three parts:
- Although I am tired
- I will finish the work
- Because I have a deadline tomorrow

<br/>


One of the most important skills is recognising that **not every direct-speech sentence has only one correct indirect-speech answer**.

Sometimes the answer depends on who is speaking, who is listening, when the sentence is reported, or what the speaker intended.

We'll learn how to identify ambiguity, explain alternative answers, and avoid marking a grammatically correct answer wrong simply because it differs from the textbook answer.

### 1. What is an ambiguous sentence?

An ambiguous sentence is a sentence that can have more than one interpretation.

Example:
```
Direct speech:  Ravi said to Mohan, "I will help you."
Indirect speech: Ravi told Mohan that he would help him.
- Who is I? Ravi.
- Who is you? Mohan.
- Here, he refers to Ravi and him refers to Mohan.

But consider this sentence:

Direct speech:   Ravi said, "He is my brother."
Indirect speech: Ravi said that he was his brother.
- This version may be grammatically correct, but the pronouns can make the meaning unclear.
- Who does he refer to?
- Who does his refer to?

The context must identify the people involved.
```

### 2. Pronouns can create ambiguity

Let's examine three examples.

Example A: `"I"` and `"you"`
```
Direct:   Meena said to Riya, "I will call you."
Indirect: Meena told Riya that she would call her.
She = Meena; her = Riya.
```

Example B: `"He"`
```
Direct: Amit said, "He is intelligent."
Indirect: Amit said that he was intelligent.
- The pronoun "he" refers to a male person mentioned or understood from the context.
- We cannot identify that person from this sentence alone.
```

Example C: `"We"`
```
Direct:   Priya said, "We are ready."
Indirect: Priya said that they were ready.
- This may be appropriate if Priya is speaking about her group from an outside reporter's perspective.
- If the reporter belongs to the group, "we are ready" may remain appropriate in a context where the group is still ready.
```

**Rule**
- Never change a pronoun without asking: **Who does this pronoun refer to?**
- Pronoun changes depend on meaning, not merely on a fixed substitution table.


### 3. Time expressions can have more than one correct form

Consider this sentence:
```
Direct speech:  Neha said, "I will finish the work tomorrow."

- If Neha said this on Monday and the report is made on Tuesday, the usual transformation is:
Indirect speech:  Neha said that she would finish the work the next day.

- But suppose the reporter is speaking on Monday, immediately after Neha's statement.
- Depending on the reporting context, tomorrow may still be used:
Indirect speech:  Neha said that she would finish the work tomorrow.
```
The time expression changes when the reporting viewpoint changes, not simply because the original sentence contains `tomorrow`.


| Direct expression	| Possible reported form	| Depends on                                 |
|-------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| `today`	          | `that day/today`	      | Reporting date                             |
| `tomorrow`	      | `the next day/tomorrow`	| Reporting date                             |
| `here`            | `there/here`	          | Speaker's and reporter's location          |
| `this`	          | `that/this`	            | What is being referred to and the viewpoint|
| `now`  	          | `then/now`	            | Whether the time reference is still current|

Important: In examination exercises, the expected answer usually assumes that the reporting takes place later and from a different viewpoint. Follow that assumption unless the question provides other context.

### 4. Tense changes can also depend on meaning

```
Direct speech: Rahul said, "I live in Delhi."
- Two possible answers are:
Indirect: Rahul said that he lived in Delhi.
Indirect: Rahul said that he lives in Delhi.

- Both can be correct.
The first is the usual past-tense transformation and reports the situation from a past viewpoint.
The second communicates that Rahul still lives in Delhi, or that the reporter is presenting this as a current fact.
```

Past viewpoint
```
Direct speech:   Rahul said, "I live in Delhi."
Indirect speech: Rahul said that he lived in Delhi.
- This reports his statement without asserting that he still lives there.
```
Current fact
```
Direct speech:   Rahul said, "I live in Delhi."
Indirect speech: Rahul said that he lives in Delhi.
- This presents the information as still true at the time of reporting.
```

The same principle applies to statements about jobs, routines, feelings, and other circumstances that may continue or change over time.


### 5. When more than one reporting verb is possible

Sometimes the same direct speech can be reported using different verbs, depending on the intention.

```
Direct speech:    Ravi said to me, "You should see a doctor."
Possible versions:
Indirect speech1: Ravi advised me to see a doctor.
Indirect speech2: Ravi suggested that I see a doctor.
Indirect speech3: Ravi said that I should see a doctor.
- All three can communicate similar advice, but their grammatical structures differ.
```
```
Direct speech:    She said, "I will help you."
Possible versions:
Indirect speech1: She said that she would help me.
Indirect speech2: She promised to help me.
Indirect speech3: She offered to help me.
```
These alternatives are not always identical in meaning:
- `Said` reports the words or message.
- `Promised` emphasises a commitment.
- `Offered` emphasises a willingness to help.

Choose the verb that best matches the context.

### 6. Ambiguity in questions

```
Direct speech:   The teacher asked Ravi, "Did you see him?"
Indirect speech: The teacher asked Ravi whether he had seen him. [Usual school transformation]

But the final "him" is ambiguous if we don't know which male person the teacher means.
To make the meaning clearer, we might write:

Indirect speech: The teacher asked Ravi whether Ravi had seen Amit.
Indirect speech: The teacher asked Ravi whether Ravi had seen the new student.
```
These versions are only appropriate if the context identifies the person that way. <br/>
Do not invent missing information in an actual transformation exercise.

Tip: Explain that indirect speech must preserve the original meaning. <br/>
It should not create new information merely to make the sentence sound clearer.

We often understand the basic rules but lose marks by making small grammatical errors. <br/>

We'll learn how to **identify the mistake, correct it, and explain why it is wrong**.

### 1. The 10 most common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Forgetting to change pronouns**
```
Direct:    Ravi said, "I am tired."
Incorrect: Ravi said that I was tired.
Correct:   Ravi said that he was tired.
- Why? "I" refers to Ravi, the original speaker.
```

**Mistake 2: Incorrect tense backshift**
```
Direct:    She said, "I am cooking."
Incorrect: She said that she is cooking.
Correct:   She said that she was cooking. [Expected in a standard school exercise]
```
- Why? A past reporting verb commonly causes the present continuous to change to the past continuous. <br/>
  The present form may still be acceptable when the context supports it.

**Mistake 3: Keeping question word order**
```
Incorrect: He asked me where was I going.
Correct:   He asked me where I was going.
```
- Why? An indirect question uses **statement word order: subject before verb**.

**Mistake 4: Using if with a WH-question**
```
Incorrect: She asked me if where I lived.
Correct:   She asked me where I lived.
```
- Why? WH-questions retain their WH-word. Use `if/whether` for yes/no questions.

**Mistake 5: Using `said` with an indirect object incorrectly**
```
Incorrect: He said me that he was busy.
Correct:   He told me that he was busy.
```
- Remember: **say something; tell someone something**. You can also **say something to someone**.

**Mistake 6: Forgetting the object in commands**
```
Incorrect: The teacher told to sit down.
Correct:   The teacher told the students to sit down.
```
- Why? In this pattern, told normally needs an object: `told + person + to + verb`.

**Mistake 7: Incorrect negative command**
```
Incorrect: Mother told me to not touched the stove.
Correct:   Mother told me not to touch the stove.
```
- Why? Use `not to + base verb`: `not to touch`, `not to run`, `not to shout`.

**Mistake 8: Changing time words without considering context**
```
Direct:   He said "I will come tomorrow."
Indirect: He said he would come the next day. [Not always correct]
- Context-dependent
"the next day" is appropriate when reporting from a later day.
- If reporting on the same day, "tomorrow" can remain.
```
- Why? Time expressions depend on when and where the report is made.

**Mistake 9: Keeping quotation marks in reported speech**
```
Direct:    She said, "I am happy."
Incorrect: She said that "she was happy."  [standard indirect-speech answer]
Correct:   She said that she was happy.
```
- Why? Indirect speech normally reports the message without quotation marks.

**Mistake 10: Using the wrong verb pattern after `"suggest"`**
```
Incorrect: He suggested me to study harder. [in standard English]
Correct:   He suggested that I study harder.
Correct:   He suggested studying harder.     [Another correct form]
```
- Why? Use `suggest + gerund` or `suggest + that-clause`, not `suggest + person + to-infinitive.`


### 2. Four more mistakes we should avoid

| Incorrect	                                                | Correct	                      | Rule               |
|-----------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|--------------------|
| ~~She asked me where did I live.~~	                    | `She asked me where I lived.`	  | Use statement word order. |
| ~~He asked that whether I was ready.~~	                | `He asked whether I was ready.`	| Do not use `that` before `if/whether`. |
| ~~She said that the Earth revolved around the Sun.~~	  | `She said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.` | Universal truths normally remain in the present tense. |
| ~~He asked me that I was happy.~~	                      | `He asked me if I was happy.` | Use `if/whether` for yes/no reported questions. |

The third example is a common school-grammar correction. In other contexts, revolved may describe a past viewpoint, but it is not the usual form when reporting a universal truth.

-   Use suggested + gerund or suggested that + clause.

12. The doctor said to him, "You should rest."
-   The doctor advised him to rest.
-   Advice can use advised + object + to-infinitive.

13. She said to me, "Why did you leave early?"
-   She asked me why I had left early.
-   Keep why, remove did as the question helper, and use statement word order.

14. He said to me, "Thank you for your help."
-   He thanked me for my help.
-   Thank you becomes thanked; your changes according to the original listener.

15. Priya said, "I live in Delhi." She still lives there when the report is made.
-   Priya said that she lives in Delhi.
-   When a statement is still true, the present tense can remain.
-   "lived" is also possible when using a past viewpoint.
```

### 4. Final learning checklist

Before learning a reported-speech transformation, ask yourself:
- [x] What type of sentence is it?
- [x] Who is speaking, and who is being addressed?
- [x] Which reporting verb fits the meaning?
- [x] Does the tense need to change?
- [x] Do the pronouns and time expressions need to change?
- [x] Does the reported question use statement word order?
- [x] Is the final sentence grammatical and does it preserve the original meaning?
